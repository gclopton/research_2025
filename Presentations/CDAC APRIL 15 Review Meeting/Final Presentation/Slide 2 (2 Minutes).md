The framework used here to reconstruct that pathway is two-temperature molecular dynamics coupled to a machine-learned interatomic potential. In the TTM-MD formulation, a continuum electronic temperature field evolves under a diffusion equation, with the ion energy deposition represented as a source term, while energy is transferred to an explicit atomistic lattice at a rate set by the electron-phonon coupling. The key modification is in the lattice model itself. Rather than using a classical fixed-charge potential, the atomic forces are supplied by an Allegro equivariant neural-network potential trained on DFT+U reference data, with the Hubbard correction applied to the Ce 4 f manifold, across the ceria reduction series $\mathrm{CeO}_2$, $\mathrm{Ce}_{11} \mathrm{O}_{20}, \mathrm{Ce}_7 \mathrm{O}_{12}$, and $\mathrm{Ce}_2 \mathrm{O}_3$.

That substitution is what makes the redox question accessible. Direct DFT cannot reach the length and time scales required for SHI track formation, while improved classical potentials do not resolve the essential limitation, because fixed-charge forms cannot represent a cerium ion that is $\mathrm{Ce}^{4+}$ in one local environment and $\mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$ in another. The distinction between those states is tied to 4f-electron localization, which is absent from a classical fixed-charge description. Extending classical TTM-MD in time or system size therefore improves sampling only within a state space that excludes the redox degree of freedom from the outset. The MLIP addresses that gap by combining near-classical MD cost with a DFT+U reference that spans the full reduction envelope, while remaining fully compatible with the TTM-MD framework.

This distinction matters because ceria is not a purely thermomechanical track-forming oxide. In systems such as $\mathrm{SiO}_2$, the dominant modeling challenge is to describe energy deposition, lattice heating, and quenching; redox flexibility is not the central issue. In ceria, by contrast, the $\mathrm{Ce}^{4+} / \mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$ degree of freedom is coupled directly to oxygen stoichiometry, vacancy formation, and oxygen transport. The cation valence state and anion sublattice therefore evolve together across the reduction series. Under a thermal spike, the resulting track morphology may depend not only on the deposited energy, but also on which local chemical state is selected during quenching. That is precisely why the reduction-series phases used in the training set are so important: they anchor the model to experimentally and electronically distinct states that span the relevant redox landscape.

Validation must therefore go beyond reproducing a single geometric descriptor. Against a canonical experimental target, such as the $946 \mathrm{MeV}{ }^{197} \mathrm{Au}$ track in single-crystal $\mathrm{CeO}_2$ characterized by TEM and EELS, the simulation should reproduce the track radius, radial density variation, $\mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$ distribution, and crystalline-to-disordered boundary within a single trajectory and without channel-specific retuning. The strongest test is then a controlled counterfactual performed within the same simulation framework: first, run the fully redox-capable MLIP; second, run a redox-suppressed comparison potential matched as closely as possible in its equilibrium bulk description. If the redox-capable model reproduces the experimental track signatures and the redox-suppressed model does not, then $\mathrm{Ce}^{4+} / \mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$ chemistry is not merely a consequence of the spike. It is an active component of the track-formation mechanism.
