# 1. What instrument does this project use to reconstruct the hidden pathway from slide 1, and what's actually new about it — not TTM-MD as such, but what has been put inside it?


Reconstructing the pathway requires a simulation framework that can carry the same hand-off the physical event itself executes — energy arriving in the electronic subsystem, flowing through electron-phonon coupling into the lattice, and driving the mechanical and chemical response of the atoms that follows. The framework that does this is two-temperature molecular dynamics. The electronic subsystem is tracked as a temperature field evolving by a diffusion equation with the ion's energy deposition entering as its source term, and the lattice is tracked as an explicit ensemble of atoms whose equations of motion are integrated directly. The two are coupled through an electron-phonon term that transfers energy from the electronic field into the atomic degrees of freedom at a rate set by the material's electron-phonon coupling strength, typically implemented as a Langevin thermostat on the atoms. TTM-MD is not new. It is a standard framework for irradiation simulation, and it is not what this project contributes.

What the framework does not specify is how the forces on the atoms are computed, and that choice, in every previous application to irradiation in oxides, has been a classical interatomic potential of fixed-charge form. For a redox-active oxide under a swift-heavy-ion quench, the chemistry that shapes the track is a chemistry of changing charges and changing bonds, and a potential whose functional form assumes neither can change cannot represent it. TTM-MD with a classical potential therefore reaches the right length and time scales but cannot carry the chemistry it is supposed to resolve.

The substitution this project makes — and the real content of what is new about the instrument — is to replace that classical potential with a machine-learned interatomic potential. The architecture is Allegro, an equivariant neural network that predicts atomic energies and forces from the local chemical environment of each atom, without committing in advance to fixed atomic charges or fixed bond lists. The reference data come from DFT+U calculations, where the Hubbard U correction is applied to the cerium f-manifold in order to recover the localized Ce³⁺ electron that standard DFT smears out. And the training set is constructed to span the ceria reduction series — CeO₂, Ce₁₁O₂₀, Ce₇O₁₂, Ce₂O₃ — so that the potential is trained on exactly the range of oxidation states and oxygen stoichiometries the thermal spike will drive the material through. The result is a single interatomic potential that is quantum-accurate in the sense of DFT+U, inexpensive in the sense of classical MD, and chemically mobile in the sense that it can follow the system through reduction and reoxidation without breaking.

Placed inside TTM-MD, this potential makes the framework, for the first time, capable of carrying the full pathway in a redox-active oxide. The instrument is not TTM-MD and it is not the MLIP in isolation; it is the coupling of the two, with each supplying what the other lacks — TTM-MD supplying the electronic energy deposition and the thermal-spike dynamics that molecular dynamics alone cannot initiate, and the Allegro MLIP supplying the interatomic forces with quantum-level redox fidelity at molecular-dynamics cost. The gap diagnosed on slide 1 — quantum accuracy on one side, relevant scale on the other — is what this substitution closes.



# 2. Why is replacing the classical potential with an Allegro MLIP trained on DFT+U data across the full CeO₂–Ce₂O₃ reduction series the specific move that makes the redox question askable, as opposed to scaling up DFT, tuning a better classical potential, or just running longer classical TTM-MD?


The redox question becomes askable only when the simulation can generate the chemistry that answers it, and that criterion is what any proposed method has to be measured against. Three routes toward that capability look plausible at first glance — scaling up DFT so that first-principles trajectories cover the needed volume and duration, tuning a better classical potential to capture the relevant chemistry, or simply running classical TTM-MD longer and over larger systems. Each fails for a specific reason, and the reasons are not the same.

Scaling DFT fails because the demand is not a factor of two or ten but many orders of magnitude in each dimension at once. The thermal spike forms in a cylindrical region of order ten nanometers across and extends through hundreds of angstroms of neighboring lattice; the quench proceeds to the nanosecond regime; and the chemistry of interest requires the Hubbard correction on the cerium f-manifold because standard DFT smears out the localized Ce³⁺ electron the redox process is about. Even with aggressive linear-scaling approximations, the combined demand of system size, trajectory length, and electronic-structure accuracy lies far outside what is computable on any foreseeable hardware. AIMD can probe small fragments of the spike; it cannot carry the whole track.

Tuning a better classical potential fails for a different kind of reason. The failure of fixed-charge pair or embedded-atom forms on redox-active oxides is not a matter of poor parameterization; it is a matter of functional form. A potential that commits in advance to a single charge per species cannot produce a cerium atom that is Ce⁴⁺ in one environment and Ce³⁺ in another, because the two states are distinguished by the localization of an f-electron that the classical form does not carry. Reactive and variable-charge variants such as ReaxFF or COMB relax this constraint partially, but none has been parameterized with quantum-level fidelity across the full CeO₂ → Ce₂O₃ envelope, and their transferability to the extremes of the spike is poor. Running the classical framework longer compounds the problem rather than repairing it: additional simulation time sharpens the statistics of an outcome drawn from a state space that does not contain the observable in question. The redox chemistry of the quench is systematically absent from the model, not imprecisely represented in it.

The machine-learned substitution closes the gap because it supplies three things simultaneously that no alternative supplies together. Scale: the potential evaluates at classical-MD cost and therefore integrates with TTM-MD at the millions-of-atoms, nanosecond range the thermal spike requires. Accuracy: the reference data are DFT+U, so the cerium f-manifold is treated correctly wherever the model is queried. Chemical coverage: the training set spans CeO₂, Ce₁₁O₂₀, Ce₇O₁₂, and Ce₂O₃, so the potential has been exposed to the full envelope of oxidation states and oxygen stoichiometries the quench can traverse, and it interpolates within that envelope rather than extrapolating outside it. Each of these ingredients existed separately before; what is specific about this substitution is that all three are present in a single object, and that object fits into the TTM-MD framework in exactly the slot where the classical potential used to sit.



# 3. Why is ceria the proof ground where that redox-capable engine earns its keep — where the question "does Ce⁴⁺/Ce³⁺ chemistry actively shape the track?" can be posed — rather than SiO₂, where the same multiscale bridge would work but the question wouldn't even arise?


The question of whether Ce⁴⁺/Ce³⁺ chemistry actively shapes the track is askable only in a material where that chemistry exists in the first place. In SiO₂, the cation is silicon, whose oxidation state is locked at Si⁴⁺ under any thermal condition the spike reaches, and the oxygen sublattice is stoichiometrically fixed. Whatever damage a swift heavy ion inflicts on fused silica is very nearly thermomechanical: bonds break, the melt resolidifies into amorphous silica, and the final track is well-described as a record of the thermal spike. A multiscale simulation with a machine-learned potential would function perfectly well there, but its redox capability would sit idle. The track would form the same way whether the potential were classical or machine-learned, because there is nothing for the added capacity to do.

Ceria sits at the opposite end of that axis. Its defining feature as a material is that the Ce⁴⁺/Ce³⁺ couple is thermally accessible and electrostatically coupled to the oxygen sublattice, so the cation oxidation state and the anion stoichiometry are not independent variables but co-vary continuously across a reduction series. The same flexibility that makes ceria a three-way catalyst and an oxygen buffer under steady-state conditions makes it, under a thermal spike, a material whose final state depends on what chemistry the quench selected. Whether that chemistry leaves an imprint on the track — whether it governs the disorder profile, the vacancy pattern, the Ce³⁺ distribution, or the density deficit — is exactly the question that could not be posed with a classical potential and that can be posed now.

Ceria also supplies the conditions under which such a question can be tested rather than merely simulated. The reduction series between CeO₂ and Ce₂O₃ passes through well-characterized intermediate phases — Ce₁₁O₂₀ and Ce₇O₁₂ among them — whose structures and formation energies have been measured and computed independently, and those same phases anchor the DFT+U training set, so the simulation is interpolating within a space whose ground truth is separately known. Swift-heavy-ion irradiation experiments on ceria already exist, and spectroscopic probes that resolve the Ce³⁺ fraction and the local oxygen environment — EELS in the TEM column, XANES at synchrotron sources — are in routine use on such samples. The computational result can therefore be compared not only against itself but against a body of structural and spectroscopic data through which predictions of redox activity either succeed or fail.

The choice of ceria is not incidental. In SiO₂, the substitution would be indistinguishable from a classical treatment and could not function as a test of the method. Ceria is the material in which the substitution is forced to do work that no classical treatment can do, and in which that work can be measured against what is already known.

# 4. What does this substitution make newly possible — what test of track physics can now be run against experiment that no prior simulation framework could run?


What the substitution makes newly possible is a comparison between simulation and experiment at the level of the chemistry, not only at the level of the track's geometry. The observables that modern microscopy and spectroscopy extract from an irradiated ceria sample — the Ce³⁺ fraction within the track, the oxygen-vacancy density in its shell, the density deficit relative to unirradiated material, the crystalline-to-disordered transition at the track boundary — are all quantities the new simulation can predict directly, because the MLIP carries the oxidation state, the oxygen stoichiometry, and the atomic positions together through the full pathway. Before this substitution, classical TTM-MD could offer a prediction of the mechanical portion of the outcome but was silent about the chemical portion. The comparison to experiment was therefore partial, running only along the variables the simulation was entitled to make statements about.

With the new instrument each of those variables becomes a simulation output. A canonical experimental target — the 946 MeV ¹⁹⁷Au track in single-crystal CeO₂, imaged by TEM with chemical characterization by EELS — becomes a multi-channel test. The simulation must reproduce the observed track radius and density profile, the measured Ce³⁺ enrichment or depletion at characteristic positions, and the crystallographic phase of the recovered lattice, all from a single trajectory whose inputs are the ion species, the energy, and the material parameters. Any single observable might be fit; the requirement to match all of them simultaneously, with no per-channel tuning, is what turns the comparison into a genuine test.

The decisive test is a counterfactual within the same framework. The simulation can be run twice: once with the full redox-capable MLIP, and once with the chemistry suppressed — either by training a comparison potential only on stoichiometric CeO₂ configurations, or by substituting a classical fixed-charge potential calibrated to the same equilibrium properties. If the redox-capable run reproduces the experimental track and the redox-suppressed run does not, the divergence between them is direct evidence that the Ce⁴⁺/Ce³⁺ chemistry is not a passive byproduct of the thermal spike but an active contributor to the track's formation. That is the question slide 1 left open — whether redox is a byproduct or a participant — and it was not posable in any earlier framework because no earlier framework could produce the "with redox" half of the comparison at the scales where the track actually forms.

The newness, then, is not in predicting tracks. Classical TTM-MD has been predicting tracks in ceria for years. The newness is in being able to ask whether the track was shaped by chemistry, and to answer that question with a simulation whose chemistry is under experimental control. The test the new instrument runs against experiment is a test of mechanism rather than of outcome, and the comparison it supports is what allows the pathway — the thing slide 1 argued had to be reconstructed — to be validated against the static state experiment measures.
