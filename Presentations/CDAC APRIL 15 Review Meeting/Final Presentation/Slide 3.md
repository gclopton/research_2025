# 1. What does the slide-2 instrument actually produce as output - a one-off track simulation, or a systematic predictive framework that generalizes across the full space of irradiation conditions (dE/dx, grain size, pre-existing damage, dopant concentration)?


What slide 2 described, read narrowly, could be mistaken for a single simulation: one ion, one material, one trajectory, one predicted track. That reading understates what the instrument is for. Because the cost of each individual run, once the MLIP has been trained, is that of classical TTM-MD, large numbers of runs are affordable. And because the MLIP's training spans the full reduction series, the potential remains valid wherever the local composition drifts during the quench, which means the range of conditions that can be explored is not limited to a single point in the irradiation phase space but extends across it.

That phase space is at least four-dimensional, corresponding to the four axes of physically meaningful variation on this slide. The electronic stopping power, dE/dx, sets how much energy enters the electronic subsystem per unit length; it is varied by changing the ion species and energy in the TTM source term. Grain size is varied by running the simulation in supercells that contain explicit grain boundaries at the relevant microstructural scale, which determines whether defects produced near the track core escape into the bulk or are trapped at grain boundaries. Pre-existing damage is varied by seeding the initial configuration with relaxed point defects, dislocations, or prior track residues, so that the framework reports how damage accumulates across repeated irradiation rather than what a single ion does to a pristine crystal. Dopant concentration is varied by substitution into the cation sublattice, with the MLIP extended as needed to cover the dopant's chemistry, so that the predicted track response reflects engineered variants of the host rather than stoichiometric ceria alone.

The output of the instrument is therefore not a track; it is a map. Each run is a point in a parameter space whose axes are the irradiation conditions, and whose predicted quantities are the track observables — track radius, density deficit, core/shell boundary, Ce³⁺ distribution, defect-type composition, and the rest. The scientific object is the surface those points define, and the single-track prediction is the smallest unit out of which that surface is assembled. The instrument qualifies as a predictive framework, rather than a case study, because the single prediction it makes is cheap enough and the underlying potential transferable enough for the space to be swept systematically.




# 2. At what scales does that framework deliver predictions — the atomic-level defect zoo, the nanoscale core/shell track morphology, and the mesoscale competition between oxygen expulsion and grain-boundary trapping - and how do those scales inform each other?


The framework delivers predictions at three scales that are not separate simulations stitched together but different resolutions of a single atomistic trajectory. The simulation operates throughout at the level of individual atoms and their forces, and what changes as the system grows is which structures the trajectory reveals. Within a few nanometers of the ion path, the relevant features are point defects and their near-neighbor clusters. Over tens of nanometers, those features organize into a track morphology. Across the full supercell, if it is large enough to contain a grain boundary, the features include the fate of the matter that tried to leave the track. Each scale is a readout of the same run, not the output of a different model.

At the atomic scale, the output is a defect zoo. Frenkel and Schottky pairs, point-defect clusters, dislocation cores, stacking faults, and twin boundaries all emerge from the MLIP's atomic-level forces without being placed in by hand. The simulation reports them as outcomes of the trajectory rather than as entries in a postulated catalog, and the identities and concentrations of each species are direct observables. The quality of this readout depends on the MLIP having been trained on configurations that sample the relevant bonding environments, which the reduction-series training set was constructed to ensure.

At the nanoscale, those atomic-level outcomes organize into a track morphology. The thermal spike is spatially structured — most energy-dense on the ion path and falling off radially — so the track inherits that structure. Within a central cylinder, the energy density reaches plasma conditions, oxygen is expelled or displaced, and the lattice melts; outside it, in a broader halo, the temperatures are lower, the damage is milder, and the lattice retains partial crystalline order. The core/shell structure is the nanoscale signature of the spike's spatial profile, and it is resolved in the same simulation that produced the defect zoo, because the morphology is just the spatial organization of the defects.

At the mesoscale, the question is what becomes of the matter that left the core. Oxygen atoms expelled from the track's interior have a finite distance to travel before they are trapped, and the grain size fixes that distance. If the supercell contains a grain boundary within that reach, the simulation resolves directly whether oxygen is retained in the bulk or exported to the boundary. The three scales inform each other because they are nested: the atomic-scale defect identities determine the density and disorder of the nanoscale morphology, the nanoscale morphology sets the spatial pattern of oxygen migration, and the mesoscale grain structure determines whether that migration terminates in the bulk or at a boundary. A single trajectory large enough to span all three scales produces all three readouts at once, and their coherence is what makes the framework predictive across scales rather than piecewise across models.



# 3. How does this connect transient, non-equilibrium SHI outcomes back to the equilibrium $\mathrm{Ce}-\mathrm{O}$ phase diagram, such that a single track simulation becomes a point on a defect phase diagram in $\mathrm{P}-\mathrm{T}-\mu$ space rather than an isolated simulation event?


The quench produces a specific frozen state — a track with its morphology, its defect population, its chemistry — and that state is not, by default, an object of equilibrium thermodynamics. It arose along a non-equilibrium pathway and stopped somewhere metastable. Reported in isolation, it is a point datum: under these irradiation conditions in this material, the simulation produced that state. To make the state a thermodynamic object, it has to be placed in a coordinate system in which equilibrium and non-equilibrium outcomes live on the same footing. That coordinate system is P-T-μ.

The anchor is the equilibrium Ce-O phase diagram, which already occupies a portion of this space. The boundaries between CeO₂, Ce₁₁O₂₀, Ce₇O₁₂, and Ce₂O₃ as a function of temperature and oxygen partial pressure are the canonical content of that diagram, and they are accessible from the MLIP because the reduction-series training set is precisely the set of phases the diagram catalogs. The equilibrium portion of P-T-μ space is, in that sense, already calibrated. The potential that generates the track trajectories is the same potential that reproduces the equilibrium phase boundaries, and the free energies that set those boundaries come from the same DFT+U reference data the MLIP inherited.

The step that makes the connection is to extend the diagram into a defect phase diagram. Each defect configuration — a particular vacancy concentration, a particular Ce³⁺ density, a particular cluster stoichiometry — has a free energy as a function of pressure, temperature, and the oxygen chemical potential μ_O, and those free energies can be computed from the same MLIP. At each point in P-T-μ space, some configuration minimizes the free energy and is the equilibrium defect phase at those conditions; other configurations are metastable with respect to it by known free-energy margins. The defect phase diagram partitions P-T-μ space into regions, each labeled by the defect structure that occupies it in equilibrium and by the metastable neighbors reachable from it.

What a single track simulation contributes is then a located point. The frozen defect population at the end of the trajectory has an effective composition and an effective residual temperature, and it sits somewhere in P-T-μ space — generally not at the equilibrium minimum for those coordinates, but at a neighboring metastable phase that the quench pathway selected. Swept across the irradiation conditions of the previous question, the individual simulations produce a distribution of such points, and that distribution is itself the object of interest: a map of which defect phases swift-heavy-ion irradiation actually accesses, overlaid on the equilibrium phase diagram the same MLIP independently reproduces. The single simulation stops being an isolated event and becomes a coordinate in a thermodynamic framework.




# 4. Why does framing the answer as a defect phase diagram — one that shows how coupled extremes shift stability boundaries between equilibrium and metastable defect phases lands the "multiple extremes" theme in a way that simulation-by-simulation cataloging of tracks would not?


A catalog of simulated tracks — one for each choice of ion, energy, grain size, dopant concentration, pre-existing damage state — is a lookup table. Each row is accurate for the conditions under which it was computed and silent about every other. Running more simulations extends the table; it does not organize it. What the enumeration cannot do is reveal the structure that makes one row related to another, or distinguish the cases where a single extreme drives the outcome from the cases where two extremes combine to drive it somewhere neither alone would reach. That structural information is exactly what the "multiple extremes" theme is about, and it is the content a catalog fails to carry.

The defect phase diagram supplies that structure because P-T-μ space is the coordinate system on which different extremes become commensurable. Shock pressure is a shift along the P axis. Thermal-spike temperature is a shift along the T axis. Oxygen expulsion and the associated depletion of the oxygen reservoir are shifts along μ_O. An irradiation event that delivers a particular combination of the three is not, in this representation, a separate entry to be indexed by ion species; it is a location in one three-dimensional space. A different event that arrives at the same location via different parameters is the same point. This is what makes extremes coupled rather than listed — they are components of a single coordinate, not rows in separate tables.

What the diagram shows, once the axes are commensurable, is how stability boundaries bend under coupled extremes. The equilibrium boundaries between CeO₂, Ce₁₁O₂₀, Ce₇O₁₂, and Ce₂O₃ are themselves curves in P-T-μ, and the metastable defect phases accessible under irradiation occupy regions defined by free-energy margins that also curve. The boundary between, say, fluorite with dilute Frenkel pairs and fluorite carrying a clustered oxygen-vacancy network is not a single point but a surface, and that surface responds differently to a rise in pressure, to a rise in temperature, or to a combined rise in both. A catalog of tracks, however densely populated, never exhibits this curvature; a phase diagram makes it the primary object.

The payoff is that the diagram becomes a common reference for other probes. An SHI simulation sits at one location in P-T-μ; a diamond-anvil high-pressure experiment sits at another; high-temperature annealing at a third; controlled-pO₂ atmosphere studies at a fourth. All are reached from different directions in the same space, and regions that no single instrument reaches alone become accessible by triangulation among them. The single track stops being an isolated simulation event and becomes an anchor within a framework in which other extremes, other instruments, and other materials can be positioned against each other. That is what "multiple extremes" means as a physics program rather than a slogan: not a list of regimes to visit, but a space whose structure the probes collectively reveal.
