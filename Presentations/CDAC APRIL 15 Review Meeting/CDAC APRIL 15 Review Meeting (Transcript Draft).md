


# Classical MD Results

We first tried to model swift-heavy-ion track formation in ceria with a classical two-temperature MD workflow in LAMMPS using a rigid-ion Buckingham + Coulomb potential. What those runs showed was not a chemically realistic track response. The simulation produced mostly thermal disorder, dispersed point defects, and local rearrangements, but it did not capture the coupled reduction-and-recovery behavior that the experimental literature suggests is active in irradiated ceria.

The reason is that the classical potential only gives us the mechanical and thermal response of a fixed-charge fluorite lattice. Real ceria is not a fixed-charge oxide under irradiation. When oxygen disorder or oxygen-vacancy formation occurs in the track core, the two excess electrons left behind localize on nearby Ce sites and create $\mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$-like centers. That redox step changes local bonding, defect formation energies, migration barriers, lattice strain, and the recovery pathway during quenching. Our rigid-ion model cannot do any of that. Every Ce is permanently constrained to the same effective charge, so the simulation is forced to treat a chemically reduced, vacancy-rich environment as if it were still sitting on the oxidized $\mathrm{CeO}_{2}$ energy surface.

That mismatch matters specifically for SHI tracks because the experiments suggest that ceria responds through coupled oxygen disorder and partial $\mathrm{Ce}^{4+}\rightarrow\mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$ reduction while retaining much of the cation framework. In other words, the material is not failing by simple melt-and-quench physics alone. The relevant physics lives in the coupling between oxygen nonstoichiometry, $4f$ electron localization, and early defect recovery. A classical fixed-charge potential can reproduce heating, elastic response, and some Frenkel-pair production, but it cannot represent the redox-driven part of the track chemistry that likely controls why ceria forms only modestly underdense, rapidly recovering tracks. That is why the classical SHI model stalled out and why we moved to a DFT+U-informed, redox-capable MLIP strategy instead of just pushing the same potential to larger cells.

# MLIP Generation

A machine-learning interatomic potential, or MLIP, is a surrogate for the quantum-mechanical potential-energy surface. Instead of prescribing a fixed analytic form with a few fitted parameters, as we do in a classical force field, we train a neural-network model on first-principles data so that it can predict total energies and atomic forces directly from the local atomic structure. In practice, that means we try to retain near-DFT fidelity for bonding and defect energetics while recovering the system sizes and time scales needed for molecular dynamics. For this project, that is exactly the gap we need to close: DFT+U can describe the redox physics of ceria, but it is far too expensive to run full SHI track simulations, while classical potentials are fast enough but miss the chemistry we care about.

The reason we are using Allegro is that it is well matched to both the physics and the scale of this problem. Allegro is an equivariant neural interatomic potential, which means it does not just look at pair distances; it also learns how the geometry of the local coordination environment transforms under rotation. That is important in ceria because the difference between oxidized and reduced environments is encoded in subtle changes in coordination, distortion, and local bonding geometry around oxygen vacancies and $\mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$-rich regions. At the same time, Allegro is strictly local and computationally efficient, which is a major practical advantage for SHI simulations. We ultimately want to run large-scale two-temperature MD in LAMMPS on systems big enough to resolve a thermal spike and its early recovery, so we need a model that is much faster than DFT and more scalable than heavier message-passing architectures. Allegro gives us that combination: high expressivity for complex local environments, good data efficiency, energy-conserving forces, and performance that is realistic for long MD trajectories.

Our generation strategy starts by defining the right quantum-mechanical reference. We will use DFT+U because the central failure mode in ceria is the treatment of the Ce $4f$ electrons and the associated $\mathrm{Ce}^{4+}/\mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$ redox chemistry. The first step is therefore to calibrate a DFT+U setup that gives a physically reasonable description of oxidized and reduced ceria, especially the placement of the $\mathrm{O}(2p)$, $\mathrm{Ce}(4f)$, and $\mathrm{Ce}(5d)$ states and the appearance of spin-polarized $\mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$ in reduced phases. Once that reference is fixed, we generate training structures across the ceria reduction series rather than only at stoichiometric $\mathrm{CeO}_{2}$. In practice that means including $\mathrm{CeO}_{2}$, intermediate oxygen-deficient phases such as $\mathrm{Ce}_{11}\mathrm{O}_{20}$ and $\mathrm{Ce}_{7}\mathrm{O}_{12}$, and the reduced end member $\mathrm{Ce}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{3}$ so that the model sees both $\mathrm{Ce}^{4+}$-rich and $\mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$-rich local environments.

From those phases, we build the actual dataset by generating relaxed structures, strained structures, vacancy-containing structures, and finite-temperature snapshots from ab initio molecular dynamics. For each configuration we compute DFT+U energies, forces, and, where useful, stresses. We then curate that dataset by removing near-duplicate structures, balancing the coverage across compositions and temperatures, and splitting it in a way that avoids leakage between similar snapshots. Allegro is then trained on those labels with a force-dominant loss so that it learns stable molecular-dynamics forces while still reproducing the relative energetics across oxidized and reduced states. After training, we validate the model on held-out configurations and on physically meaningful targets such as relative phase energetics, elastic response, and defect environments. If that validation is successful, the final MLIP becomes the atomistic engine inside our TTM-MD workflow, where we can finally test whether redox flexibility is an active ingredient in SHI track formation and early recovery rather than a passive byproduct of the spike.

# Data Generation

A major challenge in generating training data for ceria is that the difficult physics is inseparable from the electronic structure. In ceria, an oxygen vacancy is not just a missing atom in the fluorite lattice. It also leaves behind two electrons that, localize on nearby Ce sites and create $\mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$-like $4f$ states. That means our reference data must capture both the atomic distortions and the correct redox state. DFT+U can do this, but it also makes the data-generation problem harder because reduced ceria often has multiple metastable electronic solutions with different $4f$ occupation patterns. If we do not control spin polarization and initial occupations carefully, we can generate inconsistent labels for structurally similar configurations, which would make the MLIP learn a noisy and internally inconsistent energy landscape.

That is the main reason we chose to sample a specific set of ceria phases across the reduction series instead of generating data only around stoichiometric $\mathrm{CeO}_{2}$. $\mathrm{CeO}_{2}$ is the fully oxidized fluorite endpoint and gives us the baseline structure relevant to the undamaged material. $\mathrm{Ce}_{11}\mathrm{O}_{20}$ and $\mathrm{Ce}_{7}\mathrm{O}_{12}$ are intermediate oxygen-deficient phases with ordered vacancies and mixed valence, so they expose the model to local environments where $\mathrm{Ce}^{4+}$ and $\mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$ coexist. $\mathrm{Ce}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{3}$ is the reduced endpoint and anchors the strongly $\mathrm{Ce}^{3+}$-rich limit. Together, these four phases span the chemically meaningful range from fully oxidized to strongly reduced ceria. The point is not that an SHI track will exactly transform into one of these ideal bulk phases, but that these phases provide representative local motifs for the oxidized, mixed-valence, vacancy-rich, and reduced environments that the spike is likely to sample transiently.

The process we will use to generate the actual training data begins with fully relaxed cells and supercells of each of those four phases, all computed with one consistent DFT+U setup. From there, we broaden the configuration space in several controlled ways. We apply small homogeneous strains so the MLIP learns elastic response and stress behavior. We generate symmetry-distinct vacancy arrangements and locally disordered oxygen environments so the model sees a broader range of reduced coordination motifs than those present in the ideal crystal structures alone. We then run ab initio molecular dynamics at multiple temperatures to sample thermally distorted configurations, because the environments encountered during a thermal spike are strongly anharmonic and far from equilibrium. For every selected snapshot, we extract total energies, atomic forces, and, when useful, virial stresses as the labels for training.

After the raw configurations are generated, we curate the dataset before fitting the MLIP. AIMD trajectories contain many nearly identical adjacent frames, so we remove near-duplicates to avoid over-representing a narrow part of configuration space. We also rebalance the dataset across phases, temperatures, strains, and defect motifs so that the model does not become dominated by the easiest and most abundant class of data, which is usually near-equilibrium $\mathrm{CeO}_{2}$. Finally, we split the data by trajectory and structural family rather than by random frame so that the validation and test sets genuinely probe transfer to unseen local environments. The result should be a training set that is not just large, but chemically consistent and structurally representative of the oxidized-to-reduced environments that matter for redox-coupled SHI track formation in ceria.
