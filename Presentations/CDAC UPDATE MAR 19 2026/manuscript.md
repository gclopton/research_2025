## CDAC Update — March 19, 2026

# Motivation

## Radiation Damage in the Nuclear Fuel Cycle and Stockpile

The performance and longevity of nuclear materials are fundamentally limited by the damage that energetic radiation inflicts on their crystalline lattices. In operating reactors, fission fragments deposit tens of megaelectronvolts of energy along nanometer-wide tracks through the fuel pellet, creating point defects, defect clusters, and localized structural disorder that degrade thermal conductivity, promote swelling, and alter fission-gas retention [Weber et al., 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-3115(97)00401-3). These same degradation processes govern the long-term integrity of ceramic waste forms designed to immobilize surplus weapons-grade plutonium and minor actinides in geological repositories, where self-irradiation from alpha decay accumulates displacement damage over millennia [Ewing et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.1707213). In the weapons stockpile itself, plutonium pits undergo continuous alpha decay that produces 5.15 MeV alpha particles and 86 keV uranium recoil nuclei, generating Frenkel pairs, helium bubbles, and transmutation products that slowly alter the density, phase stability, and mechanical properties of the pit material [Martz and Schwartz, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11837-003-0162-z). The rate at which this damage accumulates, and the efficiency with which the lattice can heal itself through defect recombination and migration, together determine whether a given material will retain its functional properties over the required service lifetime.

Understanding and predicting these processes is central to the mission of the National Nuclear Security Administration. Under the Stockpile Stewardship Program, pit lifetime assessments must be made without underground nuclear testing, which means that predictions of long-term radiation damage evolution rely on accelerated-aging experiments, surrogate-material studies, and validated computational models [Wirth et al., 2001](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11837-001-0144-z). The largest uncertainty in current pit lifetime estimates comes from radiation damage: void swelling thresholds, helium bubble nucleation kinetics, and defect annealing rates in plutonium remain poorly constrained beyond approximately 40 years of natural aging data [Martz and Schwartz, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11837-003-0162-z). Computational and experimental tools that can compress these timescales and reveal the mechanisms controlling damage accumulation and recovery are therefore of direct programmatic value.

## Ceria as a Surrogate for Actinide Oxides

Direct experimental studies of radiation damage in plutonium dioxide are severely constrained by radiotoxicity, proliferation controls, and the specialized facilities required for handling. Cerium dioxide has emerged as the standard non-radioactive surrogate for PuO₂ and, to a lesser extent, UO₂ in radiation damage studies. The justification rests on a set of well-documented structural and chemical parallels. Cerium and plutonium have similar ionic radii (Ce⁴⁺: 0.97 Å, Pu⁴⁺: 0.96 Å), both adopt the fluorite crystal structure (space group Fm$\bar{3}$m) as their stoichiometric dioxide, and both support the same 3+/4+ valence flexibility in their oxide forms [Nelson et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/jace.13170). Fabrication studies have demonstrated that (U,Ce)O₂ and (U,Pu)O₂ mixed oxides exhibit nearly identical sintering behavior, microstructural evolution, and thermal properties above 900 K, confirming that cerium can substitute for plutonium in process development and qualification [Kim et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnucmat.2008.05.013).

![Sintered density as a function of compacting pressure for pure UO₂, U₀.₉₂Ce₀.₀₈O₂, and U₀.₉₂Pu₀.₀₈O₂. The similar pressure-density trends confirm that cerium oxide replicates plutonium oxide behavior in MOX fuel fabrication.](Papers/Actinide Context, Surrogacy and Stockpile Stewardship/Section 1: Ceria as a Surrogate for PuO2 and MOX Fuel Context/markdown_originals/Kim et al 2008 - Applicability of CeO2 as a surrogate for PuO2 in a MOX fuel development/images/db76da38-79bd-45d6-b44e-9bc82c578906-3_596_783_1571_1093.jpg)

For radiation damage studies specifically, the surrogacy extends beyond structure and thermophysics. Swift heavy ion irradiation experiments serve as controlled analogs for the damage produced by fission fragments (70-100 MeV) and alpha-recoil nuclei in real fuels, and ceria's accessibility allows systematic parametric studies that would be impractical or impossible with actinide targets. Grain size effects on irradiated CeO₂, ThO₂, and UO₂ have been compared head-to-head under identical irradiation conditions, establishing that the three fluorite oxides respond differently to the same electronic energy loss and that these differences correlate with chemical rather than purely structural properties [Cureton et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actamat.2018.08.040). The practical consequence is that ceria provides a safe, accessible experimental platform for isolating the mechanisms that control radiation tolerance in the broader family of fluorite-structured nuclear materials.

## Why Redox Chemistry Matters: The Central Hypothesis

The most important chemical property that ceria shares with plutonium dioxide — and that distinguishes both from structurally similar but redox-inert oxides like ThO₂ — is the ability to reversibly change cation oxidation state. In ceria, removing an oxygen atom from the fluorite lattice creates a vacancy and two excess electrons that localize on neighboring cerium sites as Ce³⁺ polarons, reducing the average cation valence from 4+ toward 3+. This process is not merely a bookkeeping exercise: the local bonding environment around Ce³⁺ differs measurably from Ce⁴⁺, with larger ionic radius, different elastic stiffness, and altered defect formation and migration energies. The same redox flexibility operates in PuO₂ (Pu⁴⁺ ⇌ Pu³⁺) and UO₂ (U⁴⁺ ⇌ U⁵⁺), and there is accumulating evidence that it plays an active role in determining how these materials respond to radiation.

The clearest experimental demonstration comes from Tracy et al. (2015), who irradiated ThO₂ and CeO₂ with swift heavy ions under identical conditions and found qualitatively different damage signatures. Thorium dioxide, which is monovalent (Th⁴⁺ only) and cannot undergo redox, showed conventional energy-dependent Frenkel-pair accumulation. Cerium dioxide, by contrast, exhibited an energy-independent coupled response: simultaneous Ce⁴⁺ → Ce³⁺ reduction and oxygen displacement, with lattice expansion consistent with chemical reduction rather than purely mechanical disorder [Tracy et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms7133). This result suggests that the radiation tolerance of multivalent actinide oxides is not determined solely by the thermal spike and its mechanical aftermath, but also by the efficiency of redox-coupled defect processes that can either suppress or promote damage accumulation.

This finding raises a mechanism-level question that fixed-charge atomistic models cannot address. If the Ce⁴⁺ ⇌ Ce³⁺ redox couple is merely a passive byproduct of thermal-spike heating, then conventional thermal-spike models — which predict track morphology from melting temperature and thermal diffusivity alone [Szenes, 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/0168-583X(96)00187-2) — should adequately describe ceria track formation. If, on the other hand, redox flexibility actively feeds back on vacancy energetics, migration barriers, and the thermodynamic driving forces that govern defect annealing during the quench phase, then track morphology and recovery kinetics in CeO₂ should differ systematically from what a purely thermomechanical model predicts. Distinguishing between these two scenarios is the central aim of this project, and doing so requires an atomistic model that captures both the mechanical response of the fluorite lattice and the composition-dependent energetics of the Ce–O redox chemistry across the full CeO₂ to Ce₂O₃ reduction series.

## Broader Scientific Significance

If redox chemistry does turn out to modulate track formation and recovery in ceria, the implications extend well beyond this single material system. Fluorite-structured oxides are among the most radiation-tolerant ceramics known, and they serve as hosts for nuclear fuel (UO₂, (U,Pu)O₂), inert matrix fuels for actinide transmutation, and waste forms for long-lived radionuclide immobilization [Lang et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cossms.2014.10.002). Several of these materials — including UO₂, PuO₂, and various pyrochlore compositions proposed for plutonium immobilization [Ewing et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.1707213) — share the same multivalent cation chemistry that makes ceria interesting. A demonstration that redox flexibility is a controllable knob for radiation tolerance, rather than a passive spectator, would suggest a design principle: the radiation resistance of fluorite-structured nuclear ceramics could potentially be engineered by tuning cation valence variability through composition or doping, rather than relying solely on structural criteria like bond ionicity or topological disorder tolerance [Sickafus et al., 2000](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.289.5480.748).

More fundamentally, the computational framework being developed here — a calibrated DFT+U electronic-structure reference translated into a redox-capable machine-learning interatomic potential and coupled to two-temperature molecular dynamics — represents a general methodology for treating radiation damage in any material where the local chemistry evolves under irradiation. The same approach could be applied to other redox-active ceramics, to mixed-valence perovskites, or to any system where fixed-charge potentials miss physics that matter for defect production and recovery under extreme non-equilibrium conditions.

# The Open Question

Fluorite-structured oxides share the same crystal structure and broadly similar thermomechanical properties, yet they respond to swift heavy ion irradiation in strikingly different ways. Head-to-head comparisons under identical irradiation conditions have established this point beyond reasonable doubt. Cureton et al. (2018) irradiated polycrystalline CeO₂, ThO₂, and UO₂ with the same swift heavy ion beams and found that grain-size-dependent damage accumulation differs systematically across the three materials, with CeO₂ forming a secondary reduced phase (Ce₁₁O₂₀) under irradiation that has no analog in ThO₂ [Cureton et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actamat.2018.08.040). Ishikawa et al. (2013) showed that ion track sizes in UO₂ are consistently smaller than in CeO₂ at comparable electronic stopping powers, despite the two materials having similar melting temperatures and lattice parameters [Ishikawa et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nimb.2012.03.035). If thermal properties alone governed track morphology, these materials should behave nearly identically — the fact that they do not points to a chemical degree of freedom that thermal-spike models do not capture.

![[Yablinsky et al 2015 - Characterization of swift heavy ion irradiation damage in ceria/images/3ce6b0f8-f0ed-4d20-af56-98e7fbd7383f-03_793_844_1623_1037.jpg|600]]
Figure: Electronic and nuclear stopping power for Au ions in CeO₂ as a function of ion energy. In the swift heavy ion regime (above ~100 MeV), electronic stopping dominates by more than an order of magnitude over nuclear stopping. From [Yablinsky et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1557/jmr.2015.50).

The question this project addresses is whether the critical difference is redox chemistry. Tracy et al. (2015) provided the most direct evidence by comparing the monovalent Th⁴⁺ oxide (which cannot change oxidation state) against the multivalent Ce⁴⁺/Ce³⁺ oxide under identical SHI irradiation. ThO₂ showed conventional Frenkel-pair damage that scaled with deposited energy. CeO₂ instead showed a coupled redox-structural response — simultaneous Ce⁴⁺ → Ce³⁺ reduction and oxygen sublattice rearrangement — that was largely independent of the ion energy [Tracy et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms7133). This energy-independence is the signature of a chemical mechanism rather than a purely thermomechanical one: the thermal spike deposits different amounts of energy at different beam energies, but the redox response saturates because it is controlled by defect chemistry rather than peak temperature.

# Experimental Evidence for Irradiation-Induced Reduction

Multiple independent spectroscopic techniques confirm that swift heavy ion irradiation produces genuine chemical reduction in ceria, not merely structural disorder. Iwase et al. (2009) used synchrotron XPS to quantify the Ce³⁺ fraction as a function of SHI fluence and found a systematic increase in the Ce³⁺ component of the Ce 3d core-level spectrum with increasing dose. Complementary EXAFS measurements showed a decrease in Ce–O coordination number consistent with 3-5% oxygen displacement from lattice sites — a displacement fraction too large to be explained by elastic interactions alone [Iwase et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nimb.2008.03.155). Ohno et al. (2008) independently confirmed Ce³⁺ formation at low displacement doses using both XPS and X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy, establishing that electronic excitation rather than nuclear knock-on collisions is the dominant mechanism driving the reduction [Ohno et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nimb.2007.01.292).

![[Ohno et al 2008 - Study on effects of swift heavy ion irradiation in cerium dioxide using synchrotron radiation X-ray absorption spectroscopy/images/1b48aaf2-fca1-4af9-9bd2-4d5a96b05644-3_847_841_1218_1095.jpg|500]]
Figure: Ce L₃-edge XANES spectra of CeO₂ before and after swift heavy ion irradiation, showing the emergence of the Ce³⁺ absorption feature with increasing fluence. From [Ohno et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nimb.2007.01.292).

Raman spectroscopy provides a complementary structural view. Costantini et al. (2017) tracked the evolution of Raman bands in CeO₂ single crystals irradiated with 910 MeV Pb ions and observed the growth of a defect-induced band near 600 cm⁻¹ — characteristic of oxygen vacancies in the fluorite lattice — without any loss of the F₂g first-order Raman mode, indicating that the crystal structure is retained even as the oxygen sublattice becomes increasingly defective [Costantini et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4996109). At the atomic scale, Takaki et al. (2014) used HAADF-STEM imaging to resolve individual ion tracks in CeO₂ and found that the cerium sublattice remains largely intact while the oxygen sublattice is severely disordered within a 4-5 nm track core [Takaki et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nimb.2013.04.069). Together, these results establish a consistent picture: SHI irradiation preferentially disrupts the oxygen sublattice while leaving the cation framework intact, and the resulting oxygen vacancies are compensated by Ce⁴⁺ → Ce³⁺ reduction.

# Track Morphology in CeO₂

The latent tracks left by swift heavy ions in ceria have been characterized in detail by electron microscopy. Yablinsky et al. (2015) performed comprehensive STEM analysis of CeO₂ irradiated with 945 MeV Au ions and found continuous tracks with only modest contrast changes — far less than would be expected if the material had been driven amorphous within the track core. The damage is real but limited: the fluorite lattice is retained, and the primary signature is local density reduction and oxygen sublattice rearrangement rather than wholesale structural collapse [Yablinsky et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1557/jmr.2015.50).

Quantitative measurements have established the threshold stopping power and the temperature dependence of track formation. Sonoda et al. (2008) determined that the threshold electronic stopping power for track formation in CeO₂ is approximately 15-16 keV/nm, and found that track diameters decrease by roughly 23% when the irradiation temperature is raised from room temperature to 800°C — indicating that thermal recovery is already competing with track formation at elevated temperatures [Sonoda et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nimb.2008.04.007). Yasuda et al. (2013) tracked damage accumulation as a function of fluence and found saturation at approximately 10¹⁶ ions/m² with an effective influence radius of 8.4 nm [Yasuda et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nimb.2012.12.078).

![[Rymzhanov et al 2025 - Bulk, overlap and surface effects of swift heavy ions in CeO2/images/75e74863-6e66-45b2-8a81-0ed33d285cd7-6_748_1267_1748_400.jpg|500]]
Figure: Atomistic simulation of overlapping SHI tracks near the surface of CeO₂ showing discontinuous crystalline regions separated by disordered zones in the track core. From [Rymzhanov et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnucmat.2024.155376).

On the simulation side, Rymzhanov et al. (2025) used a combined TREKIS Monte Carlo + classical MD approach to model track formation in CeO₂ under Xe ions. Their simulations predict a track core of approximately 2 nm diameter containing discontinuous crystalline regions, with 85% of defects being oxygen-related and significant recrystallization occurring within 40 ps of the initial energy deposition [Rymzhanov et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnucmat.2024.155376). These simulations use a fixed-charge potential that cannot capture redox chemistry — whether adding that capability changes the predicted track morphology and recovery timescale is precisely the question this project aims to answer.

# Annealing and Recovery: CeO₂ vs ThO₂ vs UO₂

How radiation damage recovers is as important as how it forms. Palomares et al. (2015) performed in-situ synchrotron X-ray diffraction on SHI-irradiated CeO₂ and ThO₂ inside a hydrothermal diamond anvil cell, tracking lattice parameter recovery as a function of temperature. They found that CeO₂ exhibits a two-stage recovery process with activation energies of 0.99 eV and 2.13 eV, attributed to oxygen interstitial migration and cerium vacancy migration respectively. ThO₂, by contrast, shows only a single recovery stage [Palomares et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1107/S1600576715001831). This asymmetry is directly relevant to the redox hypothesis: the multivalent Ce³⁺/Ce⁴⁺ chemistry enables additional defect migration pathways that are simply unavailable in a monovalent oxide like ThO₂.

![[Palomares et al 2015 - In situ defect annealing of swift heavy ion irradiated CeO2 and ThO2 using synchrotron X-ray diffraction and a hydrothermal diamond anvil cell/images/50a88a72-1a16-4327-bf8a-639f7a6b813f-23_587_1284_1014_416.jpg|600]]
Figure: In-situ synchrotron XRD of CeO₂ and ThO₂ samples during thermal annealing inside a diamond anvil cell. The CeO₂ and ThO₂ grains are loaded together to ensure identical temperature histories. From [Palomares et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1107/S1600576715001831).

For comparison, Weber (1983) characterized thermal recovery of lattice defects in alpha-irradiated UO₂ single crystals and identified three distinct recovery stages with activation energies of 1.5, 2.2, and 3.1 eV [Weber, 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-3115(83)90039-3). More recently, Onofri et al. (2020) used in-situ TEM to observe extended defect evolution in UO₂ during annealing from 500 to 1100°C, finding progressive dislocation loop shrinkage and annihilation [Onofri et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actamat.2020.03.005). The distinct activation energy spectra and recovery kinetics across these three fluorite oxides — all sharing the same crystal structure but differing in cation redox chemistry — strongly suggest that the chemical degree of freedom controls the defect recovery landscape.

# Competing Explanations: Why Not Just Thermal Spike?

The inelastic thermal spike model has been remarkably successful at predicting track radii and amorphization thresholds in many insulating materials using only thermophysical parameters. In this framework, the electronic energy deposited by the ion is converted to lattice heating through electron-phonon coupling, and the resulting transient temperature spike determines whether the material melts, amorphizes, or retains its crystalline structure within the track [Szenes, 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/0168-583X(96)00187-2). Toulemonde et al. (2012) showed that the inelastic thermal spike model successfully reproduces experimental track radii in many materials when the electronic and lattice thermal parameters are known [Toulemonde et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nimb.2011.06.011). For a purely thermomechanical phenomenon, this should be sufficient.

However, there are well-established alternative explanations for radiation tolerance that do not invoke redox chemistry. Sickafus et al. (2000, 2007) demonstrated that fluorite-related oxides can accommodate disorder through cation antisite formation without losing long-range crystalline order, and that this structural flexibility correlates with radiation tolerance [Sickafus et al., 2000](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.289.5480.748). Trachenko et al. (2005) proposed that bond ionicity, rather than any chemical variable, governs whether a material amorphizes under irradiation — more ionic materials resist amorphization because they can reconstruct their coordination polyhedra more easily [Trachenko et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.71.184104). In this view, ceria's radiation tolerance would be a consequence of its highly ionic bonding character, not its redox activity.

The thermal-spike and structural-flexibility explanations are not wrong — they capture real physics. The question is whether they are complete. If they were, then CeO₂ and ThO₂ should respond identically to the same irradiation (they share the same structure, similar melting temperatures, and similar bond ionicity). The experimental observation that they do not respond identically [Tracy et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms7133) implies that an additional mechanism — one that differentiates the two materials — must be operating. Redox flexibility is the most obvious candidate.

# The CeO₂₋ₓ Reduction Series

The connection between radiation damage and redox chemistry in ceria is mediated by oxygen vacancies. It is convenient to write the reduced material as CeO₂₋ₓ, where x denotes the oxygen deficiency per cerium relative to stoichiometric fluorite CeO₂. Removing an oxygen atom from the anion sublattice creates a vacancy and leaves behind two excess electrons that, in a physically faithful description, localize on nearby Ce sites to form Ce³⁺ polarons with occupied 4f character. Charge neutrality requires the average cerium valence to be 4-2x, and the Ce³⁺ fraction is approximately 2x [Tuller and Nowick, 1979](https://doi.org/10.1149/1.2129367). This linear relation makes clear why vacancy-rich configurations are also the most correlation-sensitive: each vacancy increases the local concentration of Ce³⁺ environments that carry localized 4f electrons.

Open circles denote oxygen vacancy sites.

The four representative compositions used in this work — CeO₂, Ce₁₁O₂₀, Ce₇O₁₂, and Ce₂O₃ — span this reduction series and sample the distinct local coordination motifs that the MLIP must learn. CeO₂ is fully oxidized fluorite with all Ce⁴⁺. Ce₁₁O₂₀ has an oxygen-to-cerium ratio of 20/11 ≈ 1.818, giving x ≈ 0.182 and an oxygen vacancy fraction of about 9%. Ce₇O₁₂ has O/Ce = 12/7 ≈ 1.714, giving x ≈ 0.286 and a vacancy fraction of about 14%, with a mixed-valence cation sublattice containing both Ce³⁺ and Ce⁴⁺ on crystallographically distinct sites. Ce₂O₃ is the fully reduced sesquioxide (x = 0.5) with all Ce³⁺ [Mogensen et al., 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-2738(99)00318-5). The phase diagram of nonstoichiometric CeO₂₋ₓ shows that these compositions span a region of practical relevance: reduced ceria phases appear at high temperatures and low oxygen partial pressures, precisely the conditions encountered transiently during a thermal spike [Ricken et al., 1984](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-4596(84)90113-7).

![[Pasted image 20260223092610.png|800]]
Figure: Crystal structures of the four phases in the ceria reduction series used to train the MLIP: Ce₇O₁₂ (rhombohedral, ordered vacancies), CeO₂ (fluorite), Ce₁₁O₂₀ (intermediate), and Ce₂O₃ (hexagonal sesquioxide).

For SHI thermal-spike modeling, these relations are more than stoichiometric bookkeeping. The spike can transiently disorder the oxygen sublattice and change local density, both of which alter vacancy formation and migration barriers. In a redox-capable reference, such structural changes are coupled to whether electrons localize as Ce³⁺ polarons [Nolan et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssi.2005.12.001). By explicitly including configurations drawn from CeO₂ through vacancy-rich intermediates to Ce₂O₃ in the training set, the MLIP is forced to represent the continuous spectrum of local environments implied by CeO₂₋ₓ.

# DFT+U Calibration: Methodology

The electronic structure of ceria cannot be described correctly by standard density functional theory. The partially occupied Ce 4f states in reduced ceria are strongly correlated, and the self-interaction error inherent in local and semi-local exchange-correlation functionals causes these electrons to delocalize artificially rather than forming the localized Ce³⁺ polarons observed experimentally. The DFT+U method corrects this by adding an on-site Hubbard-like penalty to the 4f orbitals that favors integer occupation and penalizes fractional filling. In the simplified rotationally invariant formulation of Dudarev et al. (1998), the correction depends on a single effective parameter U_eff = U − J rather than on U and J separately [Dudarev et al., 1998](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.57.1505).

The choice of U_eff is not free from trade-offs. Castleton et al. (2007) showed that the U value that best reproduces lattice parameters (3-4 eV) differs from the value that gives the most accurate band gap (7-8 eV), and that Ce 4f electron localization around oxygen vacancies begins at U ≈ 3 eV and reaches its maximum at approximately 6 eV [Castleton et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.2800015). Fabris et al. (2005) identified a deeper problem: DFT+U calculations for ceria admit multiple metastable electronic solutions — different patterns of 4f occupation on the Ce sites that correspond to different local minima on the DFT+U energy surface — and standard self-consistent field iterations can become trapped in the wrong minimum [Fabris et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.71.041102). Controlling which minimum is reached requires careful initialization of the occupation matrix or magnetic moments, a point that becomes especially critical when generating training data for an MLIP where every configuration must be labeled with energies and forces from the correct electronic ground state.

For this reason, our calibration spans U_eff = 4-6 eV across three compositions (CeO₂, Ce₇O₁₂, Ce₂O₃) and verifies that the chosen U_eff window preserves the qualitative physics required for redox modeling across all three: CeO₂ must remain a robust non-magnetic insulator, while reduced phases must exhibit spin polarization and near-gap Ce(4f) character consistent with Ce³⁺ formation. This strategy follows the systematic mapping approach of Loschen et al. (2007), who showed how all relevant band separations evolve with U_eff in CeO₂ and Ce₂O₃ [Loschen et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.75.035115).

# DFT+U Calibration: Results for CeO₂

Across the scanned range (U_eff = 4-6 eV), stoichiometric CeO₂ is comparatively insensitive to the Hubbard correction. The density of states retains the expected fluorite-ceria character at every U value tested: a predominantly O(2p) valence band, an insulating gap, and unoccupied Ce 4f weight appearing as the lowest Ce-derived feature above the Fermi level, with the broader Ce 5d manifold at higher energy. Consistent with this picture, the calculations remain non-magnetic (final magnetization = 0) at all tested U_eff values, confirming that the oxidized state does not spuriously localize a 4f electron. The O(2p)→Ce(4f) separation increases modestly from 2.29 eV at U_eff = 4 eV to 2.59 eV at U_eff = 6 eV, while the O(2p)→Ce(5d) separation remains approximately 5 eV and varies weakly [Loschen et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.75.035115). This weak sensitivity is helpful for MLIP reference building because the CeO₂ labels are not overly dependent on fine-tuning of the Hubbard correction.

# DFT+U Calibration: Results for Reduced Phases (Ce₂O₃, Ce₇O₁₂)

Reduced compositions exhibit the strong correlation signatures that motivate DFT+U. Both Ce₂O₃ and Ce₇O₁₂ are strongly spin-polarized, consistent with localized 4f¹ electrons on Ce³⁺ sites. The final magnetizations for Ce₂O₃ are approximately 1.9 μ_B across U_eff = 4-6 eV, close to the 2 μ_B expected for two Ce³⁺ ions per formula unit. For Ce₇O₁₂, the net moment is approximately 3.8 μ_B, consistent with its mixed-valence stoichiometry: the expected Ce³⁺ fraction of about 0.57 gives roughly four Ce³⁺ ions per seven cerium atoms.

![[Pasted image 20260223093005.png|600]]

In these reduced environments, the position and splitting of 4f features vary more noticeably with U_eff than in CeO₂. For Ce₂O₃, the O(2p)–Ce(4f) separation decreases from 3.6 eV at U_eff = 4 eV to 1.76 eV at U_eff = 6 eV, reflecting a substantial shift of the occupied Ce(4f) feature as the Hubbard correction pushes it closer to the oxygen valence band. Literature benchmarks mirror this hierarchy of sensitivity: Loschen et al. (2007) mapped how these separations evolve with U_eff, showing that the largest U-driven variations occur precisely where redox chemistry changes the near-gap electronic structure [Loschen et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.75.035115). Occupation matrix control techniques, as described by Allen and Watson (2014), were used to ensure that reduced-phase calculations consistently reached the correct electronic ground state rather than metastable configurations with different 4f occupation patterns [Allen and Watson, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1039/C3CP55432E).

# MLIP Training Strategy

The DFT+U calibration establishes the electronic-structure reference; the next step is to translate that reference into a training set for a machine-learning interatomic potential that can reach the length and time scales required for track simulations. The core requirement for a redox-capable MLIP is not an explicit charge variable but a training corpus that forces the model to learn — through geometry and local coordination alone — the distinct energetic and force responses associated with oxidized, reduced, and intermediate oxygen-deficient environments.

![[Pasted image 20260223092733.png|600]]
Figure: Workflow for redox-capable MLIP training. DFT+U reference calculations across the CeO₂₋ₓ reduction series produce labeled configurations (energies and forces). These are curated, deduplicated, and balanced across phases before training an Allegro equivariant neural network potential.

The training corpus is constructed from the same four compositions validated by the DFT+U calibration: CeO₂, Ce₁₁O₂₀, Ce₇O₁₂, and Ce₂O₃. For each composition, configurations are generated by applying homogeneous strains to teach the MLIP elastic responses, introducing oxygen sublattice disorder consistent with each stoichiometry, and running ab initio molecular dynamics with DFT+U forces to sample thermally disordered snapshots at multiple temperatures. The AIMD trajectories are curated before training: near-duplicate structures are removed using geometric similarity criteria, and the remaining configurations are balanced across phases to prevent the potential from becoming disproportionately specialized to the most frequently sampled composition [Bernstein et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41524-019-0236-6).

The architecture choice matters for redox-active oxides. Ko et al. (2021) demonstrated that purely local descriptors fail for systems where the charge distribution depends on non-local effects — specifically, two-generation and three-generation neural network potentials cannot capture long-range charge transfer because they construct atomic energies from local environment descriptors alone [Ko et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-20427-2). The Allegro architecture addresses this through equivariant message-passing on a local neighbor graph, learning rotationally equivariant features of the local environment to predict atomic energy contributions. Zhang et al. (2023) showed that even for a classical shell-model potential for CeO₂, a single model must handle Ce³⁺ and Ce⁴⁺ environments consistently across all defect charge states to produce reliable defect formation energies [Zhang et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.chemmater.2c02648). Stippell et al. (2024) recently demonstrated that DFT+U-trained MLIPs can be built for UO₂ with sufficient accuracy to reproduce defect energetics, providing a direct methodological precedent for the approach taken here [Stippell et al., 2024].

# Swift Heavy Ion Track Simulations with Two-Temperature Molecular Dynamics

To connect the electronic-stopping physics of swift heavy ions to atomistic defect production, we use a two-temperature molecular dynamics (TTM-MD) framework in which the electronic subsystem is represented as a continuum field coupled to an explicit atomistic lattice. The SHI deposits energy primarily into electronic excitations along the ion trajectory; in TTM-MD this is introduced as a spatiotemporal source term S_e(r,t) acting on an electronic temperature field defined on a coarse mesh surrounding the ion path.

![[Screenshot 2026-02-23 at 9.28.21 AM.png|600]]
Figure: Schematic of the TTM-MD framework. Left: the electronic temperature grid receives energy from the SHI source term and transfers it to the atomistic lattice through electron-phonon coupling G. Right: energy flow diagram showing the electronic subsystem, phonon subsystem, and Langevin rim boundary.

The coupled evolution of the electronic and lattice energy reservoirs follows the standard two-temperature equations. The electronic temperature T_e evolves under diffusion, electron-phonon coupling g(T_e - T_l), and the external source S_e. The lattice temperature T_l evolves through the coupling term alone, with the atomistic MD providing the microscopic pathway by which transient heating produces defects and disorder [Darkins and Duffy, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.commatsci.2017.09.049). The key physical consequence is that the electronic excitation rapidly diffuses while simultaneously heating the lattice locally, producing a transient thermal spike with large temperature gradients and strong anharmonic atomic motion near the ion path.

An important consideration for ceria is the timescale of polaron formation. Pelli Cresi et al. (2020) showed that small polaron formation in CeO₂ occurs on a timescale of approximately 330 fs after photoexcitation — fast enough to be relevant during the thermal spike, which evolves over picoseconds [Pelli Cresi et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jpclett.0c01590). This means that Ce⁴⁺ → Ce³⁺ transitions are not too slow to participate in the damage and recovery process; they occur on a timescale competitive with the lattice response itself. The electron-phonon coupling parameter g, which controls the rate of energy transfer from the electronic to the lattice subsystem, requires careful treatment in wide-gap insulators like ceria where the electronic heat capacity and conductivity differ substantially from metals [Medvedev et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.107.054101). Zeng et al. (2023) demonstrated that coupling a deep-learning potential to the TTM framework produces physically consistent dynamics for laser-driven tungsten, establishing a precedent for the MLIP+TTM integration planned here [Zeng et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41524-023-01168-4).

# What Comes Next

The DFT+U calibration presented here establishes the electronic-structure reference and confirms that it is physically faithful across the ceria reduction series. The remaining stages of the project are as follows. First, the MLIP training corpus will be generated from AIMD trajectories across all four compositions, curated, and used to train an Allegro potential. The trained potential will be validated against DFT+U benchmarks including defect formation energies, elastic constants, and phonon dispersions for both oxidized and reduced phases. Second, the validated MLIP will be coupled to the TTM-MD framework in LAMMPS and used to simulate SHI track formation in CeO₂.

The critical experiment is a controlled comparison: track simulations with the redox-capable MLIP (which can represent the continuous spectrum of Ce⁴⁺/Ce³⁺ environments) versus simulations with a fixed-charge classical potential (which cannot). If the two models produce statistically indistinguishable track morphologies and recovery kinetics, then the thermal-spike null hypothesis is sufficient and redox chemistry is indeed a passive spectator [Garrido et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nimb.2009.06.046). If the MLIP predicts systematically different track radii, defect densities, or recovery timescales, then redox feedback is an active participant — and its magnitude can be quantified by comparing the two sets of simulations against experimental track data from Yablinsky et al. (2015), Sonoda et al. (2008), and the recent simulations of Rymzhanov et al. (2025).

# Conclusion

This work establishes the first stage of a multiscale computational framework for investigating whether redox chemistry modulates swift heavy ion track formation in ceria. The DFT+U calibration across U_eff = 4-6 eV confirms that the chosen reference preserves the essential qualitative physics: CeO₂ remains a robust non-magnetic insulator, while reduced phases exhibit the expected spin polarization and near-gap Ce(4f) character associated with Ce³⁺ formation. The strongest U_eff sensitivity occurs in reduced and vacancy-rich compositions, precisely the environments that dominate the track core during and after the thermal spike. This motivates a training strategy that explicitly spans the CeO₂₋ₓ reduction series and includes oxygen-deficient, strained, and high-temperature configurations. With this calibrated reference in hand, the next steps — MLIP training, TTM-MD coupling, and the redox on/off comparison — can proceed on a foundation that has been validated against spectral and energetic benchmarks sensitive to the chemistry that this project is designed to test.
