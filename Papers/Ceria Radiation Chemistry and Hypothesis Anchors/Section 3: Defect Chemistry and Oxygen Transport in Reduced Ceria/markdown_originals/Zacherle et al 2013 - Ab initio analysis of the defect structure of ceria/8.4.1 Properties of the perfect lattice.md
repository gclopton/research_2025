# 8.4.1 Properties of the perfect lattice

  
#### 1. Why examine the calculated lattice properties of defect-free ceria?  
  
Discussion:  
The authors begin by outlining their goal: to show that their computational approach can reliably describe the “perfect” (defect-free) $\mathrm{CeO}_2$ lattice. They present calculated lattice parameters and bulk moduli for ceria using two density functional approaches (GGA and GGA$+U$) and compare these results to experimental data.  
  
* * *  
  
#### 2. How are the equilibrium lattice parameter and bulk modulus determined?  
  
Discussion:  
They obtain these quantities by fitting a Birch–Murnaghan equation of state to total energies computed at different cell volumes. This approach allows them to extract:  
  
* Equilibrium lattice parameter $(a_0)$  
* Bulk modulus $(B_0)$  
  
Both quantities are standard metrics for describing how a crystalline material responds to volumetric compression and how accurately a computational model predicts structural properties.  
  
* * *  
  
#### 3. How do the calculated lattice parameters compare to experimental values?  
  
Discussion:  
  
* GGA functional: $a_0 = 5.468\,\text{\AA}$  
* GGA$+U$ functional: $a_0 = 5.494\,\text{\AA}$  
* Experimental: $a_0 = 5.411\,\text{\AA}$  
  
Both theoretical values slightly overestimate the lattice parameter. This overestimation is a well-known characteristic of GGA-based functionals, and it tends to be even more pronounced with the inclusion of the Hubbard $U$ term (GGA$+U$).  
  
* * *  
  
#### 4. How do the calculated bulk moduli compare to the experimental value?  
  
Discussion:  
  
* GGA: $B_0 = 172\,\mathrm{GPa}$  
* GGA$+U$: $B_0 = 181\,\mathrm{GPa}$  
* Experimental: $B_0 = 220\,\mathrm{GPa}$  
  
Similar to the lattice parameter discrepancy, the calculated bulk moduli are lower than the experimentally measured value. Undercalculation of the bulk modulus is also typical of many GGA-based methods.  
  
* * *  
  
#### 5. Are the authors’ results consistent with other theoretical studies?  
  
Discussion:  
Yes. As noted in Table I of the original text, the computed values for both the lattice constants and bulk moduli align well with those reported in other theoretical work (Refs. 19, 29, 55). This consistency supports the reliability of their computational approach, despite the recognized differences from experimental values.  
  
* * *  
  
#### 6. What is the significance of the formation energy $\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}}^{\mathrm{CeO}_2}$?  
  
Discussion:  
The formation energy of $\mathrm{CeO}_2$ from its elemental constituents (metallic Ce and molecular $\mathrm{O}_2$) is a key thermodynamic quantity. It indicates how stable $\mathrm{CeO}_2$ is relative to its separated elemental forms.  
  
* * *  
  
#### 7. Why is GGA (and not GGA+U) used to compute the formation energy?  
  
Discussion:  
When dealing with metallic Ce (the element in its zero-oxidation state), there is no physical basis for adding the Hubbard $U$ correction, which targets strongly correlated electron behavior typically relevant for localized $f$\-electrons in oxidized Ce. Therefore, the authors compute $\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}}^{\mathrm{CeO}_2}$ purely with GGA. They point readers to Refs. 56 and 57 for deeper discussions on whether and when to include $U$.  
  
* * *  
  
#### 8. What corrections are applied to address the overbinding of $\mathrm{O}_2$ in GGA?  
  
Discussion:  
GGA calculations notoriously overbind the $\mathrm{O}_2$ molecule, giving an artificially low total energy. The authors mention a known correction of $1.36\,\mathrm{eV}$ per $\mathrm{O}_2$ molecule (Ref. 58). By subtracting this amount from the calculated oxidation energy, they obtain a more accurate formation energy:  
  
* Uncorrected: $\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}}^{\mathrm{CeO}_2} = -10.44\,\mathrm{eV}$  
* Corrected: $\Delta E_{\mathrm{form,corr}}^{\mathrm{CeO}_2} = -11.80\,\mathrm{eV}$ (at $T=0\,\mathrm{K}$)  
  
* * *  
  
#### 9. Does the corrected formation energy agree with experimental data?  
  
Discussion:  
Yes. The experimentally measured formation enthalpy at $T=298\,\mathrm{K}$ is $-11.28\,\mathrm{eV}$ (Ref. 59), which is close to the corrected theoretical value of $-11.80\,\mathrm{eV}$. The small discrepancy is reasonable given the difference in temperature and other possible experimental factors.  
  
* * *  
  
#### 10. What is known about ceria’s electronic structure and band gap?  
  
Discussion:  
Ceria is recognized as a wide-band-gap semiconductor, with:  
  
* Experimental band gap: $5.5-6.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ (Refs. 60, 61)  
* Valence band: predominantly $\mathrm{O}\,2p$\-derived states  
* Conduction band: primarily $\mathrm{Ce}\,5d$\-derived states  
Within the band gap, there are $\mathrm{Ce}\,4f$ states that remain unoccupied in defect-free $\mathrm{CeO}_2$.  
  
* * *  
  
#### 11. How are the Ce $4f$ states positioned relative to the valence band maximum (VBM)?  
  
Discussion:  
Experimentally, the energy difference $\Delta E_{f-\mathrm{VBM}}$ between the lowest unoccupied $4f$ level and the VBM is around $3.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ (Refs. 61, 62). These $4f$ states are thought to be localized, although experimental resolution limits make their degree of localization challenging to measure precisely.  
  
* * *  
  
#### 12. How do the authors’ calculated band gap and $4f$\-VBM separation compare to experiment?  
  
Discussion:  
Using the GGA$+U$ functional, they obtain:  
  
* Band gap: $\Delta E_g = 5.31\,\mathrm{eV}$  
* $4f$\-VBM separation: $\Delta E_{f-\mathrm{VBM}} = 2.35\,\mathrm{eV}$  
  
These results are considered “in reasonable agreement” with the experimental values (5.5–6.0 eV for the band gap and $\approx 3.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ for the $4f$\-VBM gap), demonstrating that the GGA$+U$ approach captures ceria’s electronic structure reasonably well.
