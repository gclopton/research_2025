#### 1. In what structural form does cerium dioxide (ceria) typically exist, and why is it significant?
Discussion: Ceria $\left(\mathrm{CeO}_2\right)$ predominantly adopts the cubic fluorite structure across a wide range of temperatures, hydrostatic pressures, and dopant concentrations. This structural stability makes it an attractive material for various technological applications, since its fundamental crystalline arrangement endures under diverse operating conditions.

#### 2. Why has acceptor-doped ceria attracted considerable interest?

Discussion: When ceria is doped with acceptor cations (lower valence than Ce), oxygen vacancies are created for charge compensation. These vacancies are highly mobile, resulting in high ionic conductivity. This property is particularly relevant for solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) electrolytes, where efficient oxygen-ion transport is essential for device operation.

#### 3. Why has nominally undoped ceria been comparatively under-studied, and what recent development may change this?
Discussion: Historically, most efforts have focused on improving the ionic conductivity through doping. Consequently, undoped ceria received less attention. However, recent studies have shown that undoped ceria exhibits resistive switching behavior, making it a promising candidate for use in resistive random access memory (ReRAM) devices. This discovery is expected to drive increased research into the properties and applications of undoped ceria.

#### 4. How does the intrinsic $n$-type nature of ceria manifest, and what is the role of polarons?
Discussion: Ceria behaves as an intrinsic $n$-type semiconductor, exhibiting small polaron conductivity. The polarons arise when electrons localize on cerium ions, formally transforming $\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\times}$to $\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}$. These localized electrons (polarons) significantly influence ceria's electrical behavior.

#### 5. How do oxygen vacancies compensate these polarons under different conditions?
Discussion: Under reducing conditions, electron polarons are balanced by positively charged oxygen vacancies. Specifically:
1. Low Fermi energy (high $pO_2$): Each doubly charged vacancy ( $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{* *}$ ) can compensate two polarons.
2. Higher Fermi energy: Each singly charged vacancy $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\prime}\right)$ compensates one polaron.
3. Even higher Fermi energy: Vacancies become neutral ( $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\times}$), no longer contributing to overall charge compensation.

These varying regimes underscore the need to account for multiple charge states of oxygen vacancies when modeling ceria.

#### 6. Why must both the polaronic nature of electrons and the different ionic defect charge states be considered in computational studies?
Discussion: To capture the full range of ceria's behavior-especially concerning electrical properties-one must include not only ionic vacancies (and their possible charge states) but also the localization of electrons as polarons. Earlier computational approaches often neglected one or more of these aspects (e.g., only considering neutral defect clusters, or ignoring polarons), leading to incomplete or conflicting results.

#### 7. What disagreement exists in the literature regarding the dominant defect disorder type in ceria?
Discussion: Conflicting conclusions persist about which intrinsic disorder mechanism predominates. Some computational work (e.g., by Keating et al. and Nakayama et al.) suggests Schottky disorder is lowest in energy, whereas many experimental studies indicate anti-Frenkel disorder is dominant. Resolving this discrepancy is crucial for accurate modeling and prediction of ceria's thermodynamic and transport properties.

#### 8. What is the focus of the present study, and how do the authors address the existing gaps?
Discussion: The authors use density functional theory (DFT) with the GGA $+U$ method to compute formation energies of both ionic and electronic defects in $\mathrm{CeO}_2$. Crucially, they include all intrinsic point defects in multiple charge states and pay special attention to electron polarons and their association with oxygen vacancies. This comprehensive approach aims to reconcile the disagreements in the literature and provide a more robust description of defect behavior.

#### 9. How does this study connect to experimental observations and practical applications?
Discussion: By accounting for different defect charge states and polaronic effects, the authors can describe experimentally observed phenomena, such as changes in point-defect concentrations and conductivities under varying oxygen partial pressures $\left(\mathrm{pO}_2\right)$ in both undoped and donor-doped ceria. Such insights are valuable for designing and optimizing materials for applications ranging from solid oxide fuel cells to resistive memory devices.
