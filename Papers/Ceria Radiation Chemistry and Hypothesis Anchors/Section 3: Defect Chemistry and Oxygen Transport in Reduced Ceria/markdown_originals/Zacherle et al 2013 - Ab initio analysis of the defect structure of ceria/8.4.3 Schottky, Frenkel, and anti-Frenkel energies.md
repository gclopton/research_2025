#### 3. Why do larger discrepancies arise for certain defect energies across different studies?  
  
Discussion:  
  
* Discrepancies become more pronounced for energies involving highly charged defects ($\mathrm{V_{Ce}}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}$ or $\mathrm{Ce}_i$).  
* Key reason: The work by Nakayama and Martin did not apply image-charge corrections, which are crucial in finite supercell calculations to correct for spurious electrostatic interactions between repeated defect images.  
* In the present study, image-charge corrections are significant for Frenkel ($\Delta E_{\text{corr}} = 1.94 \ \mathrm{eV}$ per defect) and Schottky ($\Delta E_{\text{corr}} = 0.77 \ \mathrm{eV}$ per defect) but are relatively small ($\Delta E_{\text{corr}} = 0.11 \ \mathrm{eV}$ per defect) for anti-Frenkel disorder.  
* When these corrections are omitted, the values in this study align well with the uncorrected values in Nakayama and Martin, underscoring the importance of applying these corrections for reliable defect calculations.  
  
  
  
#### 4. How do Keating et al.’s results compare, and what could explain their differences?  
  
Discussion:  
  
* Keating et al.$^{}29$ observed peroxide ions when placing interstitial oxygen ions but still obtained an anti-Frenkel energy similar to that calculated here.  
* However, their Schottky energy is noticeably smaller than the present result.  
* Likely cause:  
* Keating et al. put multiple defects into a single supercell, which can lead to defect interactions and associative energy effects.  
* In their anti-Frenkel calculations, the defects (vacancy and interstitial) were separated sufficiently ($d=7.17\ \mathrm{\AA}$) to avoid recombination.  
* In their Schottky calculations, the vacancies were placed close together, causing artificially lowered total energy due to defect associations.  
* Consequently, the anti-Frenkel and Schottky energies from Keating et al. do not correspond to the same “maximally charged, dilute” scenario assumed in the classical approach.
