# 8.2 THEORY

#### 1. What is the standard expression for the formation energy of an isolated charged defect?

**Answer:**  
The formation energy $\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}}(X^q)$ of a defect $X$ in charge state $q$ is given by:

$$\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}}\bigl(X^q\bigr) = E_{\text{tot,corr}}\bigl(X^q\bigr) - E_{\text{tot}}(\text{bulk}) - \sum_i n_i \mu_i + q\bigl(E_{\mathrm{Fermi}} + E_{\mathrm{VBM}}\bigr).$$

Here:

*   $E_{\text{tot,corr}}\bigl(X^q\bigr)$ is the total energy of the supercell containing the defect, corrected for finite-size effects.
*   $E_{\text{tot}}(\text{bulk})$ is the total energy of the perfect bulk cell.
*   $n_i$ is the number of atoms of type $i$ added ($n_i > 0$) or removed ($n_i < 0$) in forming the defect.
*   $\mu_i$ is the chemical potential of atom type $i$.
*   $q$ is the defect charge state, and $E_{\mathrm{Fermi}} + E_{\mathrm{VBM}}$ collectively accounts for the Fermi energy measured relative to the valence band maximum ($E_{\mathrm{VBM}}$).

* * *

#### 2. How can finite-size effects be minimized in defect formation energy calculations?

**Answer:**  
Finite-size effects (such as image-charge interactions and elastic effects) arise because supercell calculations use periodic boundary conditions, causing artificial interactions between a defect and its periodic images. To mitigate these:

1.  **Finite-size extrapolation:**  
    One can compute the formation energies using successively larger supercells and then extrapolate to the infinite supercell (i.e., infinite dilution) limit.
    
2.  **Image-charge correction:**  
    Alternatively, an image-charge correction (e.g., the Freysoldt correction) is applied to a single supercell calculation. This approach corrects for electrostatic image interactions but does not fully account for elastic interactions. It allows one to obtain a “converged” energy from just one appropriately chosen supercell.

#### 3. How is the concentration of a dilute defect calculated from its Gibbs free energy of formation?

**Answer:**  
For a defect $X^q$ with Gibbs free energy of formation $\Delta G_{\mathrm{form}}(X^q)$ at temperature $T$ and Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm{Fermi}}$, the equilibrium concentration $\bigl[X^q\bigr]$ can be described by a Boltzmann distribution:

$$\bigl[X^q\bigr] = N(X)\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{\Delta G_{\mathrm{form}}\bigl(X^q\bigr)}{k_B T}\Bigr),$$

where:

*   $N(X)$ is the concentration of available lattice sites for the defect $X$.
*   $k_B$ is Boltzmann’s constant.
*   $T$ is the absolute temperature.

* * *

#### 4. What are the key contributions to the Gibbs free energy of formation, and which can be neglected?

**Answer:**  
The Gibbs free energy of formation, $\Delta G_{\mathrm{form}}$, can be written as:

$$\Delta G_{\mathrm{form}} = \Delta E_{\mathrm{form}} + p\,\Delta V_{\mathrm{form}} - T\,\Delta S_{\mathrm{form}},$$

where:

*   $\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}}$ is the change in internal energy.
*   $p\,\Delta V_{\mathrm{form}}$ is the pressure–volume contribution.
*   $\Delta S_{\mathrm{form}}$ is the change in entropy (with $-T\,\Delta S_{\mathrm{form}}$ being the entropic contribution).

For typical ambient conditions:

1.  **Pressure–volume term:** Since the volume change $\Delta V_{\mathrm{form}}$ in forming a defect is usually small, $p\,\Delta V_{\mathrm{form}}$ can often be neglected at standard (low) pressures.
2.  **Entropy term:** While defect formation entropy $\Delta S_{\mathrm{form}}$ can matter at higher temperatures, it is often omitted for simplicity, leading to the approximation: 
    $$\Delta G_{\mathrm{form}} \approx \Delta E_{\mathrm{form}}.$$
    

Under this approximation, the defect concentration formula simplifies to:

$$\bigl[X^q\bigr] = N(X)\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}}\bigl(X^q\bigr)}{k_B T}\Bigr).$$

#### 5. How do the chemical potential and Fermi energy influence the formation energy?

**Answer:**  
From the expression

$$\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}}\bigl(X^q\bigr) = E_{\text{tot,corr}}\bigl(X^q\bigr) - E_{\text{tot}}(\text{bulk}) - \sum_i n_i \mu_i + q\bigl(E_{\mathrm{Fermi}} + E_{\mathrm{VBM}}\bigr),$$

we see two key variables:

1.  **Chemical potential $\mu_i$:** Determines how favorable it is to add or remove atoms of type $i$. Varying $\mu_i$ within the thermodynamic stability region of the material changes the defect formation energy.
2.  **Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm{Fermi}}$:** Affects the term $q\bigl(E_{\mathrm{Fermi}} + E_{\mathrm{VBM}}\bigr)$ and thus influences the formation energy of charged defects.

* * *

#### 6. How is the Fermi energy determined self-consistently via charge neutrality?

**Answer:**  
In a real material, the overall charge must balance. Hence, one imposes the charge neutrality condition:

$$\sum_{X^q} q\,\bigl[X^q\bigr] = 0.$$

*   For each choice of chemical potentials, one can solve for the Fermi energy $E_{\mathrm{Fermi}}$ that enforces this sum to be zero.
*   Once $E_{\mathrm{Fermi}}$ is fixed by charge neutrality, the concentration of each defect $\bigl[X^q\bigr]$ can then be computed from the Boltzmann expression.

#### 7. How does the oxygen chemical potential depend on the partial pressure $p\mathrm{O}_2$?

**Answer:**  
The chemical potential of oxygen, $\mu_{\mathrm{O}}$, varies with the oxygen partial pressure according to:

$$\mu_{\mathrm{O}} = \mu_{\mathrm{O}}^{\circ} + k_B T\,\ln\Bigl[\frac{p\mathrm{O}_2}{\bigl(p\mathrm{O}_2\bigr)^\circ}\Bigr],$$

where:

*   $\mu_{\mathrm{O}}^{\circ}$ is the standard chemical potential of oxygen, corresponding to the reference partial pressure $\bigl(p\mathrm{O}_2\bigr)^\circ = 1\,\mathrm{bar}$.
*   $k_B$ is Boltzmann’s constant, and $T$ is the absolute temperature.
*   This relation allows one to compute how $\mu_{\mathrm{O}}$ (and thus defect formation energies) change with different oxygen partial pressures.

* * *

#### 8. How are defect concentrations calculated as functions of temperature and oxygen partial pressure?

**Answer:**  
Using the approximations above and the relation for $\mu_{\mathrm{O}}$, one can:

1.  **Determine $\mu_{\mathrm{O}}$ from the given $p\mathrm{O}_2$.**
2.  **Find $E_{\mathrm{Fermi}}$** through the charge neutrality condition.
3.  **Compute defect concentrations** with the Boltzmann expression: 
    $$\bigl[X^q\bigr] = N(X)\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}}\bigl(X^q\bigr)}{k_B T}\Bigr).$$
    

This yields concentrations as a function of $T$ and $p\mathrm{O}_2$.



#### 9. When is the Fermi-Dirac distribution required instead of the Boltzmann approximation?

**Answer:**  
For large defect concentrations—specifically, when the defect concentration becomes comparable to the number of available lattice sites—the Boltzmann approximation (which assumes low occupation) breaks down. In such cases, one must use a Fermi-Dirac-type expression:

$$\bigl[X^q\bigr] = \frac{N(X)}{\exp\Bigl(\frac{\Delta E_{\mathrm{fom}}\bigl(X^q\bigr)}{k_B T}\Bigr) + 1}.$$

This ensures proper accounting of the limit where defect states approach full occupation. The study compares both the Boltzmann and Fermi-Dirac approaches where appropriate.
