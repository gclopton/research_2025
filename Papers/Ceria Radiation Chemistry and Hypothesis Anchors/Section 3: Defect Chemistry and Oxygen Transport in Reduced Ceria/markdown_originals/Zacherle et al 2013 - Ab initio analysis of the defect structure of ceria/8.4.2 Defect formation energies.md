
  
#### 1. What determines the chemical potential of oxygen in ceria?  
  
Discussion:  
The chemical potential of oxygen in ceria $\mu_{\mathrm{O}}^{\mathrm{CeO}_2}$ is constrained by the phase boundaries of $\mathrm{CeO}_2$. This means that when considering the stability of ceria under different conditions, the values of $\mu_{\mathrm{O}}$ must lie within the range where $\mathrm{CeO}_2$ remains the stable or coexisting phase.  
  
  
  
#### 2. Which phases define the lower and upper boundaries of $\mu_{\mathrm{O}}^{\mathrm{CeO}_2}$?  
  
Discussion:  
At low oxygen partial pressures, $\mathrm{CeO}_2$ transitions toward $\mathrm{Ce}_2\mathrm{O}_3$, setting the “oxygen-poor” boundary. Conversely, at high oxygen partial pressures, $\mathrm{CeO}_2$ is limited by the formation of gaseous $\mathrm{O}_2$, defining the “oxygen-rich” boundary. These two stable neighboring phases dictate how far the chemical potential of oxygen can vary without destabilizing $\mathrm{CeO}_2$.  
  
  
  
#### 3. What is the numerical range for $\mu_{\mathrm{O}}^{\mathrm{CeO}_2}$ under oxygen-poor and oxygen-rich conditions?  
  
Discussion:  
From the phase considerations above, the chemical potential of oxygen in ceria can vary between:  
  
* $\mu_{\mathrm{O}, \text{min}}^{\mathrm{CeO}_2} = -7.17~\mathrm{eV}$ (under oxygen-poor conditions, near $\mathrm{Ce}_2\mathrm{O}_3$),  
* $\mu_{\mathrm{O}, \text{max}}^{\mathrm{CeO}_2} = -4.93~\mathrm{eV}$ (under oxygen-rich conditions, near $\mathrm{O}_2$ gas).  
  
This range captures the extremes within which $\mathrm{CeO}_2$ remains stable relative to $\mathrm{Ce}_2\mathrm{O}_3$ and $\mathrm{O}_2$.  
  
  
  
#### 4. What information is provided in Table II regarding defect formation energies?  
  
Discussion:  
Table II lists the calculated GGA+$U$ defect formation energies for ionic point defects in both oxygen-poor and oxygen-rich environments. These calculations are performed for a Fermi energy of $0~\mathrm{eV}$. The table thus provides insight into how varying oxygen chemical potential conditions influence the energetics of different defect types in $\mathrm{CeO}_2$.  
  
  
  
#### 5. Where do interstitial defects reside in the fluorite structure of $\mathrm{CeO}_2$?  
  
Discussion:  
The interstitial defects considered in these calculations are placed in the octahedral holes of the fluorite structure. In a fluorite lattice, there are distinct interstitial sites, and the octahedral hole is typically the site of interest for interstitial defect calculations.  
  
  
  
#### 6. Why is the neutral oxygen interstitial configuration an exception?  
  
Discussion:  
Although most interstitial defects are modeled at the octahedral site, the neutral oxygen interstitial is an exception. It is found to prefer a slightly displaced configuration from the ideal octahedral site, which is energetically favored. This deviation suggests that small structural relaxations can lower the defect formation energy in certain charge states, as discussed in Section IV B3 of the referenced work.  
  
  
  
## 1. Polarons
  

  
  
#### 1. Why is the correct description of electronic defects in ceria important?  
  
Discussion:  
Ceria ($\mathrm{CeO}_2$) is known to host both localized (polaronic) and delocalized electronic states, and accurately modeling these states is essential for predicting ceria’s catalytic and transport properties. A “polaron” is a localized electron associated with a lattice distortion, whereas a “band electron” is delocalized and spread out in the conduction band. Understanding which type of defect is energetically favored is crucial for interpreting experimental results and for materials design.  
  
  
  
#### 2. How is a delocalized (band) electron introduced in the calculations, and what is its formation energy?  
  
Discussion:  
A delocalized electron is prepared by adding an extra electron to a supercell of bulk $\mathrm{CeO}_2$ without forcing any localization. This additional electron occupies one of the unoccupied $f$\-states in the band gap. The computed formation energy of this “band electron,” at a Fermi energy of $E_{\text{Fermi}} = 0 \,\mathrm{eV}$, is $\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}} = 2.20 \,\mathrm{eV}$.  
  
  
  
#### 3. Why is no charge correction possible for the band electron?  
  
Discussion:  
Because the extra electron is delocalized, it does not create a distinct, localized point charge that can be corrected using the Freysoldt \\emph{et al.} method. That method relies on evaluating monopole image-charge interactions associated with localized charges. In the case of a smeared-out (delocalized) electron, there is no well-defined, localized charge center to apply the correction to.  
  
  
  
#### 4. How is a polaron (localized electron) introduced, and why is it more challenging to prepare?  
  
Discussion:  
A polaron requires localizing the extra electron on one specific cerium ion. Simply adding an electron to the supercell does not guarantee localization—it often defaults to a delocalized solution in standard DFT calculations. To force localization, the authors initially displace neighboring ions in a way that mimics the presence of a negative point charge on the target cerium ion:  
  
* Neighboring $\mathrm{}Ce$ ions are moved \\emph{toward} the chosen $\mathrm{}Ce$ ion (where the electron is to be localized).  
* Neighboring $\mathrm{}O$ ions are displaced \\emph{away} from that $\mathrm{}Ce$ ion.  
  
By using displacements in the range $d = 1{-}2 \,\text{\AA}$ before starting the structural relaxation, the calculations successfully converge to a localized electron state on one cerium atom.  
  
  
  
#### 5. How is the localized nature of the polaron confirmed?  
  
Discussion:  
After relaxation, the partial charge density corresponding to the highest occupied state is examined (visualized using the program VESTA). The plot clearly shows that the additional electron is concentrated around one cerium ion, confirming the formation of a polaron (localized defect) rather than a spread-out band electron.  
  
  
  
#### 6. What does the density of states (DOS) reveal about the polaron vs. the band electron?  
  
Discussion:  
For the polaronic configuration, additional localized states appear within the gap between the valence band maximum and the Ce $4f$ states. These in-gap states are a hallmark of polaron formation. In contrast, the supercell containing a delocalized electron does not exhibit these localized states in the gap.  
  
  
  
#### 7. How is the formation energy of the polaron corrected, and what value is obtained?  
  
Discussion:  
Unlike the delocalized electron, the localized polaron creates a point-charge-like defect. This allows the application of the Freysoldt \\emph{et al.} correction for image-charge interactions. The correction is relatively small ($\Delta E_{\text{corr}} = 0.06 \,\mathrm{eV}$) but lowers the polaron formation energy to $\Delta E_{\text{form}} = 1.90 \,\mathrm{eV}$ (again at $E_{\text{Fermi}} = 0 \,\mathrm{eV}$).  
  
  
  
#### 8. Which electronic defect is energetically favored in these calculations, and by how much?  
  
Discussion:  
The polaron’s formation energy ($1.90 \,\mathrm{eV}$) is about $0.30 \,\mathrm{eV}$ lower than that of the delocalized band electron ($2.20 \,\mathrm{eV}$). This indicates that the polaronic (localized) state is energetically favored and therefore represents the dominant electronic defect under these calculation conditions.  
  
  
  
#### 9. How do these theoretical findings compare with experimental observations?  
  
Discussion:  
The authors note that the preference for a polaronic state over a delocalized electron aligns well with experimental results reported by Tuller \\emph{et al.} (Ref. 24 in the original text). This agreement reinforces the conclusion that in real ceria samples, localized electrons (polarons) are indeed the dominant electronic defects.  
  
  
  
## 2. Oxygen Vacancies
  

  
#### 1. Why do oxygen vacancies and polarons tend to form associates in ceria?  
  
Discussion:  
Experimental and theoretical studies ${}^{}34,64,65$ show that positively charged oxygen vacancies ($\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}$ or $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet}$) can bind with negatively charged polarons ($\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}$) to form stable complexes. This occurs because the electrostatic attraction between the $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}$ and the localized negative charge (polaron) lowers the total energy of the system. Consequently, such defect associates can be viewed as either a singly charged vacancy–polaron pair $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}-\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}\right)$ or a neutral-like vacancy–two-polaron cluster $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}-2\,\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}\right)$.  
  
  
  
#### 2. How are vacancy–polaron complexes related to formal vacancy charge states, and how are they prepared computationally?  
  
Discussion:  
From a formal perspective, $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}-\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}\right)$ behaves similarly to a singly charged vacancy ($\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet}$), and $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}-2\,\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}\right)$ similarly to a neutral vacancy ($\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\times}$). However, the electronic structure differs significantly once polarons are involved. Computationally, these complexes are formed by introducing an oxygen vacancy and carefully placing or forcing electronic charges to localize on neighboring Ce ions (as described in the previous section on polaron preparation). This ensures that the vacancy and polarons associate rather than remaining delocalized.  
  
  
  
#### 3. What distinguishes the electronic structure of a “formal” vacancy from that of a vacancy–polaron associate?  
  
Discussion:  
The partial charge densities (Fig. 2 in the original text) reveal that:  
  
* A neutral vacancy $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\times}$ distributes the extra charge equally over four nearest-neighbor Ce ions.  
* By contrast, in the $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}-2\,\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}\right)^{\times}$ associate, the extra electrons are clearly localized as two separate polarons near the vacancy.  
  
An analogous distinction is observed in the singly charged scenario:  
  
* In $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet}$, charge is equally shared by the four nearest Ce ions,  
* Whereas in $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}-\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}\right)^{-}$, the polaron is localized on one neighboring Ce ion.  
  
  
  
#### 4. How do vacancy–polaron complexes affect formation energies?  
  
Discussion:  
Formation energies of these associates are consistently lower than those of the corresponding “formal” vacancies:  
  
* The $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}-\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}\right)$ cluster is 0.14 eV lower in energy compared to $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}$.  
* The $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}-2\,\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}\right)^{\times}$ cluster is 0.47 eV lower in energy than the neutral vacancy $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\times}$.  
  
These reductions emphasize the stabilizing role of polarons in association with oxygen vacancies. However, the authors note that without careful structural preparation, the computational procedure may converge to a non-polaronic configuration (as in Fig. 2a). This underscores the need for a deliberate setup to capture polaronic states.  
  
  
  
#### 5. Why does the formation energy constrain the Fermi level in ceria?  
  
Discussion:  
Figure 3 in the original text shows the oxygen-vacancy formation energies (with or without polarons) plotted as a function of Fermi energy. If the formation energy of a particular vacancy species (e.g., $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\times}$) becomes negative at a certain Fermi level, the model would predict an unphysical violation of overall charge neutrality (no sufficient counter-charges to compensate). Consequently, the Fermi level in ceria cannot realistically fall below the point where $\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}}$ crosses zero.  
  
  
  
#### 6. What is the “negative U” effect, and how is it manifested in these calculations?  
  
Discussion:  
In many oxides, including ceria, oxygen vacancies can exhibit a negative $U$ behavior, meaning that the formation of a doubly charged or neutral vacancy can be more favorable than forming two singly charged vacancies. The calculations show:  
  
* A small Fermi energy range ($1.71$–$1.83 \,\mathrm{eV}$) where a singly charged vacancy ($\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet}$) might be favored.  
* Once polaron formation is considered, the doubly charged or neutral vacancy associates dominate, effectively suppressing the singly charged vacancy and confirming a negative $U$ effect (energetically favoring paired electrons/polarons over single ones).  
  
  
  
#### 7. How do polaron–vacancy binding energies compare with literature values?  
  
Discussion:  
The authors calculate binding energies for the clusters:  
  
* $\Delta E_{\text{bind}} = 0.33 \,\mathrm{eV}$ for the singly charged cluster $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}-\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}\right)$.  
* $\Delta E_{\text{bind}} = 0.36 \,\mathrm{eV}$ per polaron for the neutral cluster $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}-2\,\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}\right)^{\times}$.  
  
These values lie between the experimentally measured $\approx 0.20 \,\mathrm{eV}$ per polaron-vacancy bond ${}^{}64$ and other theoretical values (e.g., $0.40 \,\mathrm{eV}$ ${}^{}34$). The reasonable agreement suggests that under typical temperatures and conditions, vacancy–polaron clusters are stable and likely to form.  
  
  
  
#### 8. How do these results compare with previous theoretical work (e.g., Keating et al.)?  
  
Discussion:  
Keating \\emph{et al.} ${}^{}29$ computed a formation energy for the “oxygen vacancy” of $\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}} = 0.57 \,\mathrm{eV}$. However, as the present authors point out, that value closely matches the formation energy of the neutral cluster $\left(\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\bullet\bullet}-2\,\mathrm{Ce}_{\mathrm{Ce}}^{\prime}\right)^{\times}$, which is $\Delta E_{\mathrm{form}} = 0.56 \,\mathrm{eV}$ in an oxygen-poor environment. Thus, what might be labeled simply as an oxygen vacancy often in practice is a vacancy–polaron cluster, meaning the extra electrons are not delocalized but trapped on nearby Ce ions.  
  
  
##  3. Oxygen interstitials
 
  
  
#### 1. Why are oxygen interstitials in ceria an interesting defect to study?  
  
Discussion:  
Oxygen interstitials are extra oxygen ions occupying sites not typically part of the perfect crystal lattice. Understanding their preferred location and bonding environment is crucial for ceria’s defect chemistry because these interstitials can significantly affect ionic conductivity, redox behavior, and overall material stability.  
  
  
  
#### 2. What configuration did previous work suggest for oxygen interstitials?  
  
Discussion:  
Keating \\emph{et al.} ${}^{}29$ reported that when an oxygen interstitial is placed in the octahedral hole of the fluorite structure, it relaxes near a lattice oxygen ion, effectively forming a peroxide-like bond ($\mathrm{O}_2^{2-}$). This suggests that the interstitial $\mathrm{}O$ prefers to cluster with a host lattice $\mathrm{}O$, creating a shorter bond distance reminiscent of a peroxide species.  
  
  
  
#### 3. What are the findings for the doubly charged oxygen interstitial $\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime\prime}$?  
  
Discussion:  
Two possible structural configurations were considered:  
  
1. Peroxide-like configuration: Here, the interstitial oxygen ion is close to a lattice oxygen ion with a bond length of $d_{\mathrm{O}-\mathrm{O}} = 1.46 \,\text{\AA}$.  
2. Octahedral-site configuration: The interstitial oxygen remains in (or very close to) the octahedral hole, maintaining a larger nearest $\mathrm{}O$\-$\mathrm{}O$ distance of $2.52 \,\text{\AA}$.  
  
The authors find that the peroxide-like arrangement is actually \\emph{less} favorable (by $0.96 \,\mathrm{eV}$) than keeping the interstitial in the octahedral site. This indicates that, for $\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime\prime}$, the peroxide-type bonding is not the ground-state configuration.  
  
  
  
#### 4. What about the neutral oxygen interstitial $\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\times}$?  
  
Discussion:  
For the neutral interstitial, the most stable configuration is slightly displaced from the ideal octahedral site. Even with this displacement, the nearest $\mathrm{}O$\-$\mathrm{}O$ distance is $2.26 \,\text{\AA}$, which is still relatively large. This state is lower in energy by $0.21 \,\mathrm{eV}$ compared to the perfectly octahedral placement, but it does \\emph{not} form a peroxide-like bond (which would require a much shorter $\mathrm{}O$\-$\mathrm{}O$ distance).  
  
  
  
#### 5. Why is the peroxide-like arrangement not favored for the neutral interstitial?  
  
Discussion:  
The key factor is the large interoxygen spacing in the favored configuration. In a peroxide-like arrangement, the two oxygen atoms form a very short bond (on the order of $1.45$–$1.50 \,\text{\AA}$). Here, the distance of $2.26 \,\text{\AA}$ is significantly larger, meaning the interstitial oxygen does not strongly couple to a lattice oxygen ion. As a result, the configuration resembles a loosely bound interstitial rather than a true peroxide species.  
  
  
  
## 4. Summary


  
#### 1. What is depicted in Figure 4 and why is it important?  
  
Discussion:  
Figure 4 shows the formation energies of all the relevant ionic and electronic defects in bulk ceria plotted against the Fermi energy. This visual summary helps identify which defects become energetically favorable at different values of the Fermi level. By examining these plots, one can predict the dominant defect species under various doping or atmospheric conditions, and thus better understand ceria’s electrical and chemical properties.  
  
  
  
#### 2. What limits the Fermi energy under oxygen-poor conditions?  
  
Discussion:  
In an oxygen-poor environment, the Fermi energy is constrained by the possibility of forming oxygen vacancies and polarons. Specifically, the Fermi energy can range from $E_{\text{Fermi}} = 1.25 \,\mathrm{eV}$ up to $1.90 \,\mathrm{eV}$. Within this window, the formation energies of these defects remain non-negative, preventing unphysical predictions such as unbounded defect formation or charge imbalance.  
  
  
  
#### 3. What limits the Fermi energy under oxygen-rich conditions?  
  
Discussion:  
Under oxygen-rich conditions, two different defects—oxygen vacancies and cerium vacancies—collectively confine the Fermi energy. The permissible range in this scenario is from $E_{\text{Fermi}} = 0.09 \,\mathrm{eV}$ to $1.84 \,\mathrm{eV}$. Going below or above these bounds would lead to negative formation energies for certain defect species, which would be inconsistent with charge neutrality constraints.  
  
  
  
#### 4. Why were localized electron holes not investigated in this work, and what do existing studies indicate?  
  
Discussion:  
The authors explicitly state that they did not pursue the preparation of localized electron holes (presumably centered on oxygen ions) in their study. However, they reference Stratton \\emph{et al.}${}^{}37$, who observed that under certain conditions (low temperature, high $p\mathrm{O}_2$), the electronic conductivity of U-doped $\mathrm{CeO}_2$ scales with $\left(p \mathrm{O}_2\right)^{-1/2}$. This behavior is explained by donor dopants being compensated by clusters of localized holes and doubly charged oxygen interstitials. Formally, such clusters can be written as:  
  
$$\left[D^{\prime}\right] = \left[\left(\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\prime\prime}-\mathrm{h}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}\right].$$  
  
Although not a focus here, these observations show that under some doping and environmental conditions, localized holes can play a significant role in ceria’s defect chemistry.
