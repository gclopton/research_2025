> [!abstract] Purpose
> A research-centered, self-directed graduate curriculum supporting a program in **multiscale modeling of radiation effects in ceria and related oxides**: electronic structure of correlated oxides (DFT+U), defect chemistry and nonstoichiometry, swift-heavy-ion track formation, coupled transport, and machine-learned interatomic potentials. The curriculum defines 87 **course-equivalent modules** across eight area documents. Each module carries a six-part Resources section (defined below) to be filled with lecture series, seminars, web courses, books, reviews, and foundational papers.

# How to Use This Curriculum

## Design Principles

1. **Modules, not degrees.** The unit of study is the *course-equivalent module* — a body of material narrow enough to describe exactly what is needed, but broad enough to support a coherent sequence of lectures and textbook chapters. Do not reproduce entire graduate degrees in physics, chemistry, mathematics, nuclear engineering, or machine learning.
2. **Area documents are filing categories.** The eight area documents organize the curriculum; they do not define it. A module lives in the document representing its main purpose and cross-references supporting modules elsewhere. Link — never duplicate content across documents.
3. **The research-centered test.** For every proposed module ask: *Will this body of knowledge repeatedly help me formulate, implement, validate, or interpret research in my program?* If yes, it belongs. If it might be useful only once, it goes in a reference list, not the four-year core. If it is intellectually adjacent but unlikely to affect the work, omit it.
4. **Harvest real courses selectively.** Given a 30-lecture course, first map its lecture titles against the module's objectives; watch only the central lectures and supplement with selected lectures from other courses and chapters from monographs. A module is assembled, not enrolled in. This is not cutting corners — a conventional course serves a broad population; this curriculum is optimized around a specific scientific problem.
5. **Modules may cross disciplinary boundaries.** That reflects the actual science (e.g., small polarons in ceria draws on solid-state physics, quantum chemistry, defect chemistry, transport, spectroscopy, and DFT+U). File each module by its main purpose and cross-link the rest.
6. **NPTEL is a primary source.** Its catalog contains specialized graduate courses whose titles are close to many modules here (*Solid State Physics*, *Bonds and Bands in Solids*, *Foundations of Computational Materials Modelling*, *Diffusion in Solids*, *Defects in Crystalline Solids*, *Phase Field Modelling*, *Fundamentals of X-Ray Diffraction and TEM*, ML for materials, and more). Known leads are noted per module in the area documents.

## Priority Levels

- **A — Core course.** Most of a strong graduate course is relevant; treat as something close to a complete course.
- **B — Focused course-equivalent.** Construct from selected lectures and chapters drawn from one or more courses.
- **C — Research specialization.** Usually learned from monographs, summer schools, conference tutorials, review articles, and isolated lecture series, after the prerequisites.

Split ratings (A/B, A/C, B/C) mark modules with a core component at the first level and specialized extensions at the second.

## Resource Types

Every module in the area documents carries a `#### Resources` section with six subsections. Replace the — placeholder under each as material is found.

- **Video Lecture Series** — full lecture series (actual courses) on YouTube or similar platforms: a playlist of videos covering all lectures within a course. Prefer the YouTube version of a playlist whenever the same series is available there, because YouTube transcripts can be extracted for AI-assisted note-making. If no YouTube version exists, use the official non-YouTube video series page.
- **Video-Based Research Presentations and Seminars** — recorded seminars and research presentations by individual researchers, plus summer-school and conference tutorials. The place for specialized research topics that are usually not covered in a course.
- **Web-Based Lecture Courses** — full courses with all materials for each lecture as text (notes, slides, assignments); there may or may not be a corresponding video series for each lecture.
- **Books and Monographs** — foundational textbooks and specialized graduate books.
- **Review Articles and Tutorials** — written tutorial reviews and pedagogical survey articles: literature that *teaches* an area of research rather than reporting a single result. Especially important for C-level modules.
- **Foundational Research Papers** — primary-literature papers foundational to the area, worth reading directly given the topics the module emphasizes.

When recording any resource, note the *specific* lectures or chapters that map to the module — a module is assembled from selected parts, not consumed whole (Design Principle 4). Track prerequisites and cross-references with wikilinks, and completion status in the Module Index below.

## Resource Formatting

Once resources have been found, format each resource subsection as a Markdown table, not as a prose list. Leave the single `—` placeholder only when no usable resource has been selected yet. Order entries by learning value for the module: main backbone resources first, then focused supplements, then optional or advanced resources. Do not sort alphabetically unless that also matches the recommended study order.

Use the `Resource` column for a Markdown link to the resource title. Prefer official source pages for books, course pages, articles, and papers; for video lecture series, prefer the YouTube playlist when an equivalent YouTube version exists, and put the official course/source page in `Notes` if it is useful. Avoid bare URLs.

Use `Best use in this module` to say the role of the resource, not to summarize the resource generally. Use `Recommended coverage` for the exact lectures, weeks, chapters, sections, or papers that map to the module. Use `Notes` for caveats: what to skip, whether the resource is a backbone or supplement, transcript/source-page comments, prerequisites, and cross-references to other modules.

Use these table structures by subsection:

| Resource subsection | Table columns |

| Video Lecture Series | `Resource` · `Instructor / provider` · `Best use in this module` · `Recommended coverage` · `Notes` |
| Video-Based Research Presentations and Seminars | `Resource` · `Presenter / venue` · `Best use in this module` · `Recommended coverage` · `Notes` |
| Web-Based Lecture Courses | `Resource` · `Instructor / provider` · `Best use in this module` · `Recommended coverage` · `Notes` |

Keep table entries compact enough to scan inside Obsidian. If a resource needs extensive commentary, put only the actionable guidance in the table and create a separate note for detailed reading notes.

# The Document Set

The curriculum is a set of nine documents. This one is the master index; the eight area documents below hold the modules. Module numbers encode their document — module 4.8 is the 8th module of [[04. Computational and Numerical Methods]].

- [[01. Physics]] — Electronic structure and correlation, lattice dynamics, statistical mechanics, and response/excitations. *(10 modules)*
- [[02. Materials Science and Solid-State Chemistry]] — Crystallography and crystal chemistry, defects and nonstoichiometry, thermodynamics, diffusion, transformations, and interfaces. *(13 modules)*
- [[03. Applied Mathematics]] — Linear and functional analysis, variational and symmetry methods, transforms and PDEs, probability, dynamics, and optimization. *(10 modules)*
- [[04. Computational and Numerical Methods]] — Numerical foundations, atomistic and electronic-structure algorithms, and computing practice (HPC, verification, reproducibility). *(14 modules)*
- [[05. Continuum Transport and Mechanics]] — Conservation laws, heat and mass transport, coupled/irreversible transport, and track-relevant multiphysics. *(9 modules)*
- [[06. Radiation–Matter Interaction and Radiation Effects]] — Stopping, collision cascades, electronic excitation and track structure, thermal spikes, radiation chemistry, codes, and experiments. *(12 modules)*
- [[07. Machine Learning for Atomistic Materials]] — ML foundations, geometric/equivariant learning, machine-learned interatomic potentials, and scientific ML. *(11 modules)*
- [[08. Experimental Characterization and Validation]] — Diffraction and imaging, spectroscopy, irradiation and transport measurements, and quantitative analysis. *(8 modules)*

# Where the Broad Subjects Actually Go

Traditional course subjects are deliberately *not* modules. Their useful fractions are distributed as follows.

## Electrochemistry

Do **not** create a general electrochemistry module. Its useful material lives in: 2.5 Defect Chemistry and Nonstoichiometric Oxides · 2.6 Solid-State Ionics and Mixed Conductors · 5.4 Irreversible Thermodynamics and Coupled Transport · 8.7 Thermal and Transport Measurements. Needed: electrochemical potential, activity, Nernst relations, ionic transport, mixed conduction, space charge. Not needed: exhaustive aqueous cells, batteries, electrode manufacturing.

## General Thermodynamics

Do not retake undergraduate thermodynamics. The advanced material is divided among: 1.8 Statistical Mechanics and Phase Transitions · 2.7 Thermodynamics of Materials and Chemical Potentials · 2.8 Phase Diagrams and Grand Potentials · 5.4 Irreversible Thermodynamics · 4.9 Free-Energy Methods.

## General Quantum Mechanics

Do not repeat a standard graduate quantum-mechanics sequence unless a gap becomes obvious. The next steps are: 1.1 Quantum Theory of Solids · 1.3 Many-Body Quantum Mechanics · 1.4 Strongly Correlated Electrons · 1.5 Crystal Fields and f-Electron Physics · 1.2 DFT.

## General Fluid Mechanics

Do not take a complete fluid-mechanics curriculum merely because transport appears in the project. Use: 5.1 Continuum Conservation Laws · 5.2 Heat Conduction · 5.3 Reaction–Diffusion · 5.4 Irreversible Thermodynamics · 5.5 Kinetic Theory.

## Nuclear Engineering

Do not reproduce a nuclear-engineering degree. Use: 6.1 Radiation–Matter Interaction · 6.2 Ion–Solid Interactions · 6.4 Radiation Damage · 6.5 Electronic Stopping · 6.6 Electron Cascades · 6.8 Swift-Heavy-Ion Track Formation.

## General Machine Learning

Do not spend most of the time on image classification, language models, or generic business datasets. After 7.1 Statistical Learning Fundamentals, move quickly to: 7.3 Graph Neural Networks · 7.4 Equivariant Learning · 7.5 Atomistic Representations · 7.6 MLIPs · 7.7 Active Learning and Uncertainty · 7.8 Redox-Aware and Long-Range Models.

# Curriculum Size and Expectations

This curriculum contains roughly **25–30 A-level modules** (justifying something close to a complete course), **25–30 B-level modules** (constructed from selected lecture sequences and chapters), and **10–15 C-level specializations** (studied after the relevant foundation).

That is large, but it is not equivalent to taking ~70 conventional semester courses: a B-level module may use six lectures from one course, four from another, and three chapters from a monograph; a C-level module may be a review article, a summer-school series, and selected monograph chapters. The organization also prevents wasted time on conventional course material that belongs to a parent discipline but not to this research.

Over four years, the realistic target is: **deep command of the A-level modules**, **strong working knowledge of the B-level modules**, and **selective mastery of the C-level modules** that become directly relevant to the dissertation.
